# B2B Positioning Frameworks for ContextOps

## Framework portfolio and ranking

For a B2B positioning system, I would **not select one framework and discard the others**. The strongest sources address different layers of the positioning problem. April Dunford is unusually strong at selecting a competitive context; Kevin Lane Keller adds the often-missed distinction between *points of parity* and *points of difference*; Category Entry Points add buying situations and mental availability; Bain deepens the definition of customer value; Jobs-to-be-Done improves the needs model; Category Design is useful for the exceptional case in which the existing category itself is wrong; and Andy Raskin provides a downstream narrative architecture. citeturn21view3turn20view0turn16view3turn16view0turn16view1turn16view2turn22view0

The scores below are my assessment, not published efficacy scores. **Evidence quality** evaluates the quality of the underlying source base, while **ContextOps fit** evaluates how cleanly the framework can be converted into deterministic questions and run against accumulated company, customer, competitor, market, and product context.

| Framework | Primary job | Positioning completeness | B2B fit | Evidence quality | ContextOps fit | Recommendation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **April Dunford — Five Components** | Find the most advantageous competitive position | 5/5 | 5/5 | 4/5 | 5/5 | **Core framework** |
| **Keller — Frame + POPs + PODs + Mantra** | Build a defensible brand position | 5/5 | 4/5 | 5/5 | 5/5 | **Core validation layer** |
| **Category Entry Points — Romaniuk / Ehrenberg-Bass** | Connect the brand to buying situations | 3.5/5 | 5/5 | 5/5 | 5/5 | **Mandatory complement** |
| **Bain B2B Elements of Value** | Identify what value actually matters | 3/5 | 5/5 | 4.5/5 | 5/5 | **Strong value layer** |
| **JTBD / Outcome-Driven Innovation** | Define customer jobs and unmet outcomes | 3/5 | 4.5/5 | 4/5 | 5/5 | **Strong customer-needs layer** |
| **Category Design** | Reframe or create the category itself | 4/5 | 4.5/5 | 3/5 | 4/5 | **Situational, not default** |
| **Andy Raskin Strategic Narrative** | Turn the position into a change story | 2.5/5 | 5/5 | 3/5 | 4.5/5 | **Activation layer** |
| **Moore-style positioning statement** | Compress the strategy into a compact artifact | 2.5/5 | 4.5/5 | 4/5 | 4.5/5 | **Output format, not discovery method** |

Two distinctions are particularly important for your Second Brain:

**Positioning discovery ≠ positioning statement.** Dunford explicitly criticizes the traditional fill-in-the-blanks statement because it presupposes that you already know the right target, category, competitors, and value proposition. Keller independently makes a similar criticism: conventional statements can overemphasize one differentiator, neglect competitor advantages and fail to provide a growth platform. citeturn21view3turn20view0

**Positioning ≠ messaging.** Dunford treats positioning as the strategic input from which messaging, storytelling, branding, and related execution follow. That distinction is extremely useful for ContextOps because it means your system should first infer and validate a *position*, and only then generate language. citeturn17search9turn21view4

My recommended architecture is therefore:

> **Buyer reality → Competitive reality → Value → Strategic position → Memory structure → Narrative → Messaging**

The frameworks below map naturally to those layers.

## April Dunford — Five Components of Positioning

**Verdict: the best default core framework for B2B positioning discovery.**

Dunford decomposes positioning into five interdependent elements: **competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, customer value, target-customer segmentation, and market category**. Crucially, she gives them a causal order. Start with what customers would do if the solution did not exist; identify capabilities the alternatives lack; translate those capabilities into customer value; identify the customers who care disproportionately about that value; and finally choose the market category or frame that makes the value easiest to understand. citeturn21view3

That ordering is what makes the method particularly attractive for ContextOps. It is not merely a canvas containing five empty boxes. Each answer constrains the next answer:

**Alternatives → Differentiated capabilities → Differentiated value → Best-fit customers → Best market frame.** citeturn21view3

The most strategically important concept is probably **competitive alternatives**. Dunford defines the starting question in practical terms as what customers would do if your product did not exist. That forces the analysis beyond named category competitors to include manual processes, spreadsheets, internal solutions, adjacent categories, doing nothing, or maintaining the status quo. citeturn21view3

The second major strength is the **market frame of reference**. Declaring a category causes buyers to infer expected capabilities, competitors, value and even pricing conventions. Dunford therefore treats category selection as a strategic choice rather than a descriptive taxonomy exercise. She explicitly notes that the same offering can often be framed in multiple markets and that creating an entirely new category is the most difficult positioning option. citeturn21view4

**Strengths.** This is the most operational framework in the set. It forces differentiation to be **relative to an alternative**, value to be **caused by a capability**, segmentation to be **based on who values the differentiation**, and category selection to be **justified by the preceding analysis**. That makes hallucinated positioning considerably easier to detect: an unsupported assertion breaks one of the links in the chain. This assessment follows directly from the dependencies Dunford describes among the components. citeturn21view3

**Weaknesses.** Its center of gravity is product/solution positioning rather than the complete long-term problem of building a B2B corporate brand. It tells you exceptionally well **what competitive context to choose**, but much less about the range of buying situations in which buyers should retrieve the brand from memory. That is the gap filled by Category Entry Points. Dunford's published methodology is also primarily practitioner-derived rather than peer-reviewed academic positioning research, so I would rate its practical evidence highly but its formal evidence below Keller or the Ehrenberg-Bass work. citeturn21view3turn16view3

**ContextOps recommendation:** make this the **primary inference engine**. Do not let the model jump directly from “customer problem” to “positioning statement.” Require it to build and evidence the complete Dunford chain first.

## Keller — Competitive Frame, Points of Parity, Points of Difference, Brand Mantra

**Verdict: the strongest framework for validating whether a proposed position is actually viable as a brand position.**

Kevin Lane Keller's brand-positioning model defines four elements of superior competitive positioning:

**competitive frame of reference → points of difference → points of parity → brand mantra.** citeturn20view0turn8view1

The framework overlaps with Dunford but adds something strategically important: **you do not win merely by being different**.

Keller's **Points of Difference (PODs)** are associations buyers see as positively valuable and not available to the same extent from competitors. A candidate POD needs to pass three tests: it must be **desirable to the customer, deliverable by the company, and differentiating from competitors**. citeturn20view1turn20view2

His **Points of Parity (POPs)** solve the other half of the problem. Category POPs are the associations required for the offering to be regarded as a credible member of the chosen category. Competitive POPs neutralize advantages competitors would otherwise possess. Keller explicitly argues that PODs alone are insufficient when important POPs are missing. citeturn20view2

This is particularly useful in B2B. A disruptive SaaS company, for example, may be radically better on automation but still lose consideration if enterprise buyers doubt security, integrations, service, implementation or governance. The concrete examples here are illustrative adaptations of Keller's POP principle rather than Keller's own B2B list. The principle itself is that category legitimacy and competitor neutralization must accompany differentiation. citeturn20view2

Keller's fourth component, the **brand mantra**, is an internal rather than external slogan: a short articulation of what the brand should represent and, implicitly, what it should not represent. Keller recommends that it communicate the brand's domain and uniqueness, remain simple enough to be remembered, and inspire employees. citeturn20view3

A particularly useful ContextOps artifact is a **POP/POD matrix**:

| Attribute / association | Buyer importance | Category requirement? | Competitor strength | Our strength | Role |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| X | High | Yes | High | High | Category POP |
| Y | High | No | High | Comparable | Competitive POP |
| Z | High | No | Low | High | POD candidate |
| A | Low | No | Low | High | Irrelevant uniqueness |

That final row matters. **Uniqueness is not automatically differentiation.** Keller's criteria require relevance/desirability as well as uniqueness and deliverability. citeturn20view1

**Strengths.** Compared with Dunford, Keller provides the better **position quality-control system**. Dunford asks, “What differentiated value can we put at the center?” Keller adds, “What must also be true for the buyer to believe that we are a legitimate option?” He also explicitly connects target-market choice to the buyer's consideration set and competitive frame. citeturn20view1turn21view3

**Weaknesses.** It is not inherently B2B-specific, and it does not provide Dunford's equally explicit causal discovery sequence. A team can still populate the boxes with management opinions unless customer, competitor and market evidence is required. Keller himself stresses that the competitive frame requires understanding customer behavior and consideration sets, reinforcing the need for external evidence rather than workshop intuition alone. citeturn20view1

**ContextOps recommendation:** run Keller **after Dunford**. Dunford discovers the candidate position; Keller attacks it.

A useful automated gate is:

> **No position passes unless it has a credible Frame + POP set + defensible POD set.**

## Category Entry Points — Ehrenberg-Bass / Jenni Romaniuk / LinkedIn B2B Institute

**Verdict: the most important modern corrective to a positioning system that becomes too obsessed with owning one narrow idea.**

Category Entry Points, or **CEPs**, represent the buying situations that cause buyers to mentally enter a category. The B2B Institute's work with the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute explicitly describes CEPs as a means of linking brands to the buying situations that matter and provides processes for identifying, prioritizing and measuring them. citeturn16view3

This creates an important tension with traditional positioning.

Classical positioning often asks:

> “What single differentiated idea should this brand own?”

CEP thinking asks:

> “In which relevant buying situations should this brand come to mind?”

Romaniuk's position is therefore broader than simply finding one association. Marketing Week's discussion of the work emphasizes that CEPs are buyer/category phenomena, not brand inventions: marketers need to understand the situations that matter in the category and then develop brand associations with the relevant ones. citeturn1search23

The B2B framework proposes two particularly useful mechanisms. First, use “the W's” to uncover buying situations. Second, prioritize them through the **3Cs: Commonness, Credibility and Competition**—eliminating situations that are uncommon, ones in which the brand lacks credibility, or ones that are already highly competitive. citeturn16view3

That makes CEPs exceptionally compatible with ContextOps because they can be represented as structured objects rather than vague personas.

**Strengths.** CEPs solve an important blind spot in many differentiation-led frameworks: a brand can have a sharp position and still fail to be retrieved when a purchase situation emerges. The LinkedIn/Ehrenberg-Bass B2B work explicitly links CEP strategy to building mental availability and provides identification, prioritization and measurement mechanics. citeturn16view3turn1search27

It is also unusually well suited to B2B because buying needs often arise from **situations rather than demographics**. A CIO does not continuously need a migration platform; a trigger such as a legacy-system deadline, merger, cost mandate or architecture change can make the category suddenly salient. Those examples are my B2B translation of CEP logic, not a claim that those exact triggers appear in the source framework.

**Weaknesses.** CEPs are **not a complete positioning framework**. They can tell you *when the brand should come to mind* without fully resolving *why this brand should be chosen over alternatives*. That is precisely why I would not substitute CEPs for Dunford or Keller. I would combine them. citeturn16view3turn21view3turn20view1

This yields a useful distinction for your Second Brain:

> **Positioning:** Why should this buyer choose us in this competitive context?  
> **CEPs:** In which buying contexts should the buyer remember that we exist?

That is one of the most important architectural distinctions in the entire framework stack.

## Bain — B2B Elements of Value

**Verdict: the best supplementary framework for preventing shallow “benefits” from being mistaken for B2B customer value.**

Bain's B2B Elements of Value framework emerged from analysis of quantitative and qualitative customer studies conducted over three decades. Bain identified **40 elements of B2B value** organized into five levels: **table stakes, functional value, ease of doing business, individual value and inspirational value**. The work was also published in *Harvard Business Review*. citeturn16view0

Its significance for positioning is that B2B value is not reducible to ROI and product functionality. Bain's work specifically argues that subjective and personal considerations can matter alongside price, specifications, compliance and total cost; it gives buyer reputation and reduced anxiety as examples. citeturn16view0

This is a very useful antidote to a common B2B positioning failure:

> Feature → generic benefit → “increase efficiency.”

The Bain lens forces a much richer question:

> **Exactly what form of value is created, for whom, and at what level?**

| EOV-05 | Does it increase revenue, reduce cost, improve quality, reduce risk, improve performance or enable scale? | Economic mechanism |

| EOV-07 | **How does the offering make doing business easier?** | Ease-of-business value |

| EOV-09 | Does the relationship, expertise, responsiveness or service model itself create value? | Relationship value |
| EOV-10 | **What personal value does choosing this solution create for individual stakeholders?** | Individual value |

| EOV-20 | Which small set of value elements combines high importance, high company performance and meaningful competitive advantage? | Positioning candidates |

Questions EOV-15 through EOV-18 are a **ContextOps extension** rather than a formal new layer in Bain's pyramid. They adapt Bain's explicit observation that B2B value matters to different important stakeholders to the realities of buying groups. Bain itself states that its 40 elements can be used to identify what matters to different sets of stakeholders. citeturn16view0

A particularly useful output is a **value ladder**:

```text
Capability
   ↓
Functional consequence
   ↓
Business outcome
   ↓
Organizational value
   ↓
Individual stakeholder value
   ↓
Evidence
```

For example, the point is not merely:

> “Automated reporting.”

It is to establish a chain such as:

> automated reporting  
> → less manual reconciliation  
> → shorter monthly close  
> → lower operational cost / faster decision making  
> → lower anxiety and greater confidence for the responsible executive  
> → supported by customer evidence.

The example is illustrative; the framework basis is Bain's distinction between objective, ease-of-business, individual and inspirational value. citeturn16view0

**Strengths.** Extremely useful for B2B positioning because it pushes the model beyond feature-benefit clichés and recognizes both organizational and human value. It is also based on a much broader research base than a single consultant's anecdotal method. citeturn16view0

**Weaknesses.** It does **not decide the position for you**. Forty possible value elements can actually generate sprawling positioning unless constrained by competitive differentiation. The correct combination is therefore:

> **Bain finds potentially important value → Dunford asks whether it is caused by differentiated capabilities → Keller asks whether it is desirable, deliverable and differentiating.** citeturn16view0turn21view3turn20view1

That combination is considerably more robust than simply asking an LLM, “What is our value proposition?”

## Jobs-to-be-Done and Outcome-Driven Innovation

**Verdict: best used as a customer-needs and segmentation substrate beneath positioning—not as the final positioning framework.**

At its core, Jobs-to-be-Done reframes demand around the progress or “job” customers are trying to accomplish rather than around the product itself. Strategyn's formulation states the central idea directly: people buy products and services to get jobs done. Its Outcome-Driven Innovation methodology then connects jobs to customer needs, opportunity identification, segmentation, competition and positioning. Strategyn's published body of work includes a 2002 *Harvard Business Review* article, the 2008 HBR “Customer-Centered Innovation Map,” and work in *MIT Sloan Management Review*. citeturn16view1

For positioning, the most useful element is **outcome-based segmentation**. Rather than assuming customers are different because they sit in different industries or company-size bands, Strategyn proposes identifying segments according to which desired outcomes are unmet and how those needs differ. citeturn16view1

This fits extraordinarily well with Dunford's question:

> “Who cares a lot about our differentiated value?”

JTBD/ODI gives you a better mechanism for answering *why* some customers care more than others. Dunford herself notes that JTBD influenced her decision to begin the positioning process with competitive alternatives. citeturn21view3

### ContextOps translation

| ID | Question | Required output |
|---|---|---|
| JTB-01 | **What fundamental job is the customer trying to get done, independent of our solution?** | Core job |
| JTB-02 | How would the customer describe the job without using vendor or category terminology? | Customer job language |
| JTB-03 | What makes this job arise repeatedly over time? | Job stability |
| JTB-04 | What event makes the job urgent enough to seek a different solution? | Trigger |
| JTB-05 | What solutions, processes or workarounds does the customer currently “hire” to get the job done? | Current alternatives |
| JTB-06 | Where does the current approach fail or require trade-offs? | Struggle points |
| JTB-07 | **What outcomes define doing the job well?** | Desired outcomes |
| JTB-08 | What does the customer want to increase, decrease, accelerate, prevent, simplify or make more predictable? | Outcome direction |
| JTB-09 | Which outcomes are especially important? | Importance |
| JTB-10 | Which important outcomes are poorly satisfied by existing alternatives? | Unmet outcomes |
| JTB-11 | Which outcomes are already over-served and therefore poor differentiation targets? | Over-served outcomes |
| JTB-12 | **Do groups of buyers share distinct patterns of unmet outcomes?** | Outcome segments |

| JTB-14 | Which segment's unmet outcomes align most strongly with our differentiated capabilities? | Positioning segment |

For your system, JTBD should generate a **needs graph**, not a tagline:

```text
Job
 ├── Context
 ├── Trigger
 ├── Existing alternatives
 ├── Desired outcomes
 │    ├── Outcome A
 │    ├── Outcome B
 │    └── Outcome C
 ├── Unmet outcomes
 ├── Segment variation
 └── Evidence
```

Then connect this to Dunford:

```text
Unmet outcome
      ↓
Customer segment that cares disproportionately
      ↓
Our differentiated capability that solves it
      ↓
Differentiated value
      ↓
Best competitive frame
```

**Strengths.** JTBD protects the positioning process from becoming overly product- or firmographic-centric. It is particularly useful when your knowledge base contains transcripts, customer interviews, support conversations, win/loss analysis and sales-call data from which jobs, situations and desired outcomes can be extracted. Strategyn's methodology explicitly extends from needs and opportunity analysis into outcome-based segmentation and positioning. citeturn16view1

**Weaknesses.** “JTBD” is a broad family of ideas rather than one universally standardized marketing-positioning process. The Strategyn/ODI implementation is therefore best treated as one well-developed school, not as the sole legitimate interpretation of Jobs-to-be-Done. More importantly, knowing the customer's job still does not answer the complete positioning question: multiple competitors can address the same job. You still need comparative alternatives, capabilities, category and POD/POP analysis. citeturn16view1turn21view3turn20view1

**ContextOps recommendation:** JTBD is a **pre-positioning ontology**. Use it to structure customer need/context before asking the positioning engine to make strategic choices.

## Category Design and Strategic Narrative

These two frameworks are valuable, but I would deliberately keep them **outside the default core positioning algorithm**. They become useful under specific conditions.

**Category Design: use when the competitive frame itself must change.**

Category Design practitioners associated with *Play Bigger* frame the work around topics such as the problem, category/solution, differentiation, “universal truth,” point of view, category blueprint, ecosystem, persona, mobilization and “lightning strikes.” The discipline explicitly aims at defining and developing a new category rather than merely finding a better position inside an existing one. citeturn16view2

This makes Category Design more radical than Dunford's normal category selection. Dunford allows new-category creation but calls it the most difficult positioning option; Category Design makes that exceptional move the center of the strategy. citeturn21view4turn16view2

### Category Design questions

| ID | Question | Required output |
|---|---|---|
| CAT-01 | **Is the company's value difficult to understand because the current category frame is genuinely wrong?** | Reframing case |
| CAT-02 | What important change in technology, economics, regulation, behavior or organizational practice has made an old problem newly important? | Market change |
| CAT-03 | What problem does the existing category fail to describe adequately? | Problem definition |
| CAT-04 | Can that problem be expressed independently of our product? | Category-level problem |
| CAT-05 | Is there evidence that multiple customers already experience or recognize this problem? | Market evidence |
| CAT-06 | What existing categories currently absorb the budget or attention? | Existing frames |
| CAT-07 | Why are those existing categories structurally inadequate? | Category gap |
| CAT-08 | **What new category would make the problem and solution easier—not harder—to understand?** | Category hypothesis |
| CAT-09 | Can the category be explained in plain buyer language without lengthy education? | Comprehensibility |
| CAT-10 | What rules or evaluation criteria define a good solution in this category? | Category rules |
| CAT-11 | Does our product uniquely satisfy those rules, or are we simply renaming an existing market? | Authenticity test |
| CAT-12 | Which competitors could legitimately join the category? | Ecosystem |
| CAT-13 | Which analysts, partners, customers, media or communities would need to adopt the category concept? | Ecosystem mobilization |
| CAT-14 | Does the company have enough resources and patience to educate a market rather than borrow an existing category's meaning? | Execution feasibility |
| CAT-15 | **Would an existing category or subcategory communicate our differentiated value almost as well at dramatically lower education cost?** | Kill criterion |
| CAT-16 | What evidence would cause us to abandon the category-creation hypothesis? | Falsification criterion |

CAT-15 is perhaps the most important ContextOps question. Category creation is seductive because it makes every company sound revolutionary. Dunford's framework provides the necessary counterweight: use the frame that makes the differentiated value obvious, and recognize that creating a completely new category is the hardest route. citeturn21view4

**Evidence assessment:** Category Design has a substantial practitioner methodology and reported consulting experience, but the framework's public evidence base is more practitioner/case-driven than the research foundations behind Keller, CEPs or Bain. I would therefore treat it as a **high-upside strategic hypothesis that must carry a higher burden of proof**, not as the default answer for an innovative company. citeturn16view2

**Andy Raskin Strategic Narrative: use after positioning to turn the strategy into a story.**

Raskin's well-known narrative architecture consists of five sequential moves:

> major change in the world → winners and losers → Promised Land → capabilities as “magic gifts” → proof.

His central move is to begin with an external change that creates stakes and urgency, rather than starting with the company or product. The “Promised Land” is the customer's desirable future state, explicitly not the vendor's product. Product capabilities then become enabling mechanisms, followed by proof such as relevant customer success. citeturn22view0turn22view2turn22view3turn22view4

### Strategic Narrative questions

| ID | Question | Required output |
|---|---|---|
| NAR-01 | **What important external change is reshaping the customer's world?** | Change |
| NAR-02 | Is the change independently verifiable, or merely a convenient marketing assertion? | Evidence |
| NAR-03 | Why does the change matter now? | Urgency |
| NAR-04 | What becomes harder, riskier or less effective under the old approach? | Old-world consequence |
| NAR-05 | What are organizations that adapt doing differently? | New behavior |
| NAR-06 | Are “winner/loser” claims supported by evidence rather than rhetorical exaggeration? | Narrative integrity |
| NAR-07 | **What desirable future state can the customer reach by adapting?** | Promised Land |
| NAR-08 | Can the Promised Land be described without mentioning our product? | Customer-centricity test |
| NAR-09 | What measurable characteristics define that future state? | Success criteria |
| NAR-10 | Which of our differentiated capabilities enable the transition? | Enablers |
| NAR-11 | How does each capability connect causally to the Promised Land? | Capability → outcome |
| NAR-12 | What customer cases prove that the transition is achievable? | Social proof |
| NAR-13 | What technical or operational evidence proves the claims? | Product proof |
| NAR-14 | Does the narrative reinforce our chosen positioning, or quietly imply a different category and competitive frame? | Alignment |
| NAR-15 | Can sales, marketing, executives and product teams tell the same strategic story? | Organizational consistency |

Raskin argues that the narrative works best when the wider organization aligns around the same story, not merely when sales adopts a better deck. citeturn22view0turn22view4

**Position in the stack:** this is **not where ContextOps should discover the positioning**. It is where ContextOps should *activate* an already defensible position.

That order prevents a dangerous failure mode:

> compelling story → weak strategic truth.

The safer direction is:

> evidence → positioning → narrative.

## Recommended ContextOps positioning operating system

Rather than making one framework your entire positioning method, I would construct the Second Brain around a **composite positioning decision system**. The frameworks are sufficiently complementary that each can serve as a check on the others. Dunford provides competitive causality, Keller provides strategic viability, CEPs provide buying-situation breadth, Bain provides value depth, JTBD provides needs structure, and Category Design/Raskin provide exceptional reframing and narrative activation respectively. citeturn21view3turn20view1turn16view3turn16view0turn16view1turn16view2turn22view0

I would structure it as the following pipeline.

| ContextOps stage | Framework | Decision being made | Core output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Buyer reality** | JTBD + CEP | What causes demand and what is the customer trying to accomplish? | Jobs, outcomes, triggers, buying situations |
| **Competitive reality** | Dunford + Keller | What does the buyer actually compare us against? | Alternatives, consideration set, competitive frame |
| **Differentiation** | Dunford | What can we do that alternatives cannot? | Differentiated capabilities |
| **Value architecture** | Bain + Dunford | Why do those differences matter? | Capability → value chains |
| **Segment selection** | Dunford + JTBD | Who cares disproportionately? | Best-fit segment / anti-ICP |
| **Position selection** | Dunford | In what market context is our value most obvious? | Market frame |
| **Position validation** | Keller | Are we credible enough and different enough? | POPs + PODs |
| **Demand-memory strategy** | CEPs | When should the buyer remember us? | Priority CEPs |
| **Category escalation** | Category Design | Is the existing category fundamentally inadequate? | Existing / subcategory / new-category decision |
| **Compression** | Keller + Moore-style statement | Can the strategy be explained compactly? | Positioning document + brand mantra |
| **Activation** | Raskin | How should the position become a compelling strategic story? | Narrative |
| **Validation** | Customer/market research | Does the intended market perceive it as we do? | Evidence, contradictions, iteration |

The ordering is important. Dunford explicitly separates positioning from messaging, while Keller's framework similarly treats the position as strategic guidance rather than merely a slogan. citeturn21view4turn20view0

### The master ContextOps question set

If I had to reduce all of the research into **one canonical question schema** for running against your collected context, I would use the following.

**Buyer and situation**

1. What fundamental job is the customer trying to accomplish?
2. Which outcomes define success?
3. Which important outcomes are currently poorly satisfied?
4. What event, change or circumstance causes the customer to enter the market?
5. What are the most important recurring buying situations?
6. Who becomes involved in each situation?
7. How does the customer describe the problem before encountering vendor language?

This combines JTBD's jobs/outcomes lens with the buying-situation logic of Category Entry Points. citeturn16view1turn16view3

**Competitive reality**

8. What would customers do if this company did not exist?
9. Which alternatives are products, internal processes, manual approaches, adjacent categories or status quo?
10. What consideration set does the buyer actually construct?
11. Why does the buyer prefer each alternative?
12. Which alternative wins most often, and under what circumstances?

The emphasis on the buyer's real alternatives follows Dunford, while the consideration-set framing comes directly from Keller. citeturn21view3turn20view1

**Differentiation and proof**

13. What capabilities do we possess that important alternatives do not?
14. Which claimed differentiators are actually category table stakes?
15. Which are meaningfully superior rather than merely different?
16. What evidence proves each differentiator?
17. How durable is each advantage?
18. Which “advantages” appear only in internal company language but not customer evidence?

Dunford provides the alternative-relative differentiation logic; Keller adds the test that a real POD must be desirable, deliverable and differentiating. citeturn21view3turn20view1

**Value**

19. What customer outcome does each differentiated capability cause?
20. What functional/economic value results?
21. What friction or ease-of-doing-business value results?
22. What individual stakeholder value results?
23. What strategic or inspirational value results?
24. Which value elements matter to which members of the buying group?
25. Which value elements are important but undifferentiated?
26. Which are important **and** differentiated?

This combines Dunford's capability-to-value dependency with Bain's multi-level B2B value architecture. citeturn21view3turn16view0

**Best-fit customer**

27. Which customers care disproportionately about the differentiated value?
28. What observable characteristics identify them?
29. Which situational characteristics predict fit better than firmographics?
30. Which unmet outcomes make them especially receptive?
31. What trigger makes the value urgent?
32. Who looks like the ICP demographically but lacks the conditions that create strong value?
33. What is the resulting anti-ICP?

Dunford explicitly recommends narrowing the target to those prospects that care especially strongly about the differentiated value, while outcome-based segmentation supplies an additional way to identify those groups. citeturn21view4turn16view1

**Market frame**

34. Which market categories could plausibly contain this offering?
35. What assumptions will buyers make under each?
36. Which competitors appear under each frame?
37. Which POPs become mandatory under each frame?
38. Under which frame are our most defensible strengths central to the purchase?
39. Under which frame do weaknesses become disqualifying?
40. Which frame makes the value easiest to understand?
41. Could a subcategory provide more useful context?
42. Is there truly sufficient evidence to justify creating a new category?

Dunford explicitly treats category as a context that affects buyer assumptions about value, competitors, features and pricing, while Keller's frame-of-reference model connects category to target market, close substitutes and the consideration set. citeturn21view4turn20view1

**Parity and difference**

43. What must a credible provider in this category be able to claim?
44. Where do we fail those expectations?
45. What competitor advantages need to be neutralized?
46. What are our strongest candidate PODs?
47. Is each desirable?
48. Is each consistently deliverable?
49. Is each meaningfully differentiating?
50. Is each believable and provable?
51. Which combination of PODs and POPs produces the strongest defendable position?

These questions operationalize Keller's POP/POD model. citeturn20view1turn20view2

**Mental availability**

52. In which buying situations should the brand come to mind?
53. How common is each situation?
54. How credible is the brand in each?
55. How intense is competitive association with each?
56. Which CEPs deserve priority?
57. Which useful buying situations are missing from the current positioning/message system?
58. Is the position unnecessarily restricting the number of legitimate situations in which the brand could be considered?

The Commonness–Credibility–Competition prioritization logic is part of the B2B CEP framework. citeturn16view3

**Strategic narrative**

59. What external change makes this position more important now?
60. What is objectively changing rather than merely being asserted by the company?
61. How does the customer need to behave differently?
62. What does the desirable future state look like?
63. Can that future state be described without mentioning our product?
64. Which capabilities enable the transition?
65. What evidence shows that we can make the transition happen?

These map to Raskin's change → winners/losers → Promised Land → enabling capabilities → evidence structure. citeturn22view0turn22view2turn22view4

### Decision rules for the Second Brain

I would hard-code several rules rather than leave them to open-ended generation:

**A differentiator cannot exist without an alternative.**

> `Differentiated Capability X` is valid only if the system can answer:  
> **Different from which alternative, in what respect, based on what evidence?**

That directly follows Dunford's dependency between competitive alternatives and differentiated capabilities. citeturn21view3

**A feature cannot become positioning merely because it is unique.**

> It must create valuable customer consequences and survive Keller's **desirable + deliverable + differentiating** test. citeturn20view1

**A benefit cannot become a POD merely because customers value it.**

> If every credible competitor delivers it, classify it as a **Point of Parity**, not a Point of Difference. citeturn20view2

**An ICP should not be selected independently of differentiated value.**

> The preferred target is the segment with conditions that cause it to value the differentiated capability disproportionately. This is central to Dunford's sequencing and compatible with ODI's outcome-based segmentation. citeturn21view3turn16view1

**A category should not be inherited from company history.**

> Generate multiple plausible frames and ask which makes the company's differentiated value easiest for the best-fit customer to understand. Dunford explicitly warns against defaulting to the market in which the product originated. citeturn21view4

**A new category must pass a much higher evidence threshold.**

> Existing category → subcategory → new category should be an escalation path, not three equally attractive stylistic choices. Dunford explicitly identifies new-category creation as the most difficult option, while Category Design demonstrates the much broader organizational and ecosystem work involved in deliberate category creation. citeturn21view4turn16view2

**Positioning breadth and memory breadth should not be confused.**

> A brand can have a coherent strategic position while linking itself to multiple relevant Category Entry Points. The CEP framework specifically seeks wider and fresher networks of relevant buying-situation associations rather than reducing the brand to one purchase cue. citeturn16view3turn1search23

**A positioning statement should be generated last.**

Traditional positioning statements can be useful as a compression device, but both Dunford and Keller identify substantial shortcomings when the template substitutes for the strategic work. Geoffrey Moore's positioning work likewise emphasizes target market, competition and a compelling value proposition; that makes Moore-style syntax more useful as an **output artifact after discovery** than as your primary discovery algorithm. citeturn21view3turn20view0turn21view2

**Messaging should be downstream of positioning.**

Dunford explicitly separates the two: positioning provides inputs from which messaging is then developed. Raskin's strategic narrative fits naturally at this downstream stage. citeturn21view4turn22view0

### Recommended framework hierarchy for ContextOps

The resulting hierarchy I would encode in your Marketing Second Brain is:

> **CORE POSITIONING ENGINE**  
> April Dunford  
> `Alternatives → Differentiated Capabilities → Value → Best-fit Customers → Market Frame`
>
> **POSITION QUALITY GATE**  
> Keller  
> `Frame → Category POPs → Competitive POPs → PODs → Brand Mantra`
>
> **BUYER-CONTEXT LAYER**  
> JTBD + Category Entry Points  
> `Jobs → Outcomes → Triggers → Buying Situations`
>
> **VALUE DEPTH LAYER**  
> Bain B2B Elements of Value  
> `Functional → Ease → Individual → Inspirational`
>
> **MEMORY / DEMAND LAYER**  
> Category Entry Points  
> `Buying Situation → Commonness × Credibility × Competition → CEP priority`
>
> **CATEGORY ESCALATION PATH**  
> Category Design  
> `Use only when existing categories materially obscure the value`
>
> **ACTIVATION LAYER**  
> Andy Raskin  
> `Change → Stakes → Promised Land → Enablers → Proof`

The most powerful aspect of this architecture is that **no individual framework is allowed to answer a question it is weak at**. Dunford determines competitive positioning but does not have to solve mental availability. CEPs identify buying situations but do not have to prove differentiation. Bain enriches value but does not select the market. JTBD discovers needs but does not decide why one provider wins. Keller validates parity and difference but does not need to create the sales story. Category Design is only invoked when category selection fails. Raskin narrates an already-supported strategy rather than inventing one. The boundaries follow the explicit scope and mechanics of the respective frameworks. citeturn21view3turn20view0turn16view3turn16view0turn16view1turn16view2turn22view0

For a ContextOps system, I would therefore rank the practical implementation priority as:

**April Dunford → Keller → Category Entry Points → Bain → JTBD/ODI → Category Design conditional branch → Raskin activation.**

That produces something materially stronger than a conventional positioning canvas: an **evidence-backed positioning reasoning graph** in which every strategic conclusion can be traced backward from position → value → capability → customer need → competitive alternative → source evidence.
