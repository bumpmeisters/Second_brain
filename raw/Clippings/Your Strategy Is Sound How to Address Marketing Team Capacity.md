# Your Strategy Is Sound: How to Address Marketing Team Capacity

> Quelle: https://www.heinzmarketing.com/blog/your-strategy-is-sound-how-to-address-marketing-team-capacity/
> Veröffentlicht: 2026-07-16T18:24:34

## Summary

Marketing leaders are being asked to deliver more with leaner teams, making it difficult to know whether stalled work is caused by an unclear strategy or a lack of execution capacity. This article helps leaders diagnose what is actually getting in the way and determine whether they need strategic guidance, additional support to get the work done, or a combination of both.

*By [Maria Geokezas](https://www.linkedin.com/in/mgeokezas/),Chief Operating Officer at Heinz Marketing*

Most marketing leaders we talk with are not short on ideas. They have plans, priorities, campaigns, and executive support. Their teams know which audiences they need to reach and what they need to say.

The problem is that too much of the work is sitting in Asana or still on a roadmap instead of getting to market because marketing team capacity has not kept pace with expectations.

A campaign manager leaves or goes on parental leave. A major product launch gets added to an already full calendar. Sales needs immediate support for a new opportunity. Leadership decides the company must develop its AI capabilities, but none of the existing priorities go away. Everyone is busy, yet the work most likely to create pipeline keeps slipping.

When this happens, the natural reaction is often to revisit the strategy. Leaders schedule another planning session, reconsider the campaign calendar, or ask the team to improve its processes. Sometimes that is necessary. But sometimes the strategy is not the problem at all. The team simply does not have the capacity to execute it.

Recognizing the difference matters. If the real issue is capacity, more planning can make things worse. It adds meetings and decisions to a team that is already stretched while doing nothing to move the work forward.

## Start by asking what is actually stuck

A marketing capacity problem does not always look like people openly saying they have too much work. More often, it appears as a collection of smaller execution problems. Campaigns take weeks longer to launch than expected. Reviews and approvals become bottlenecks. Important projects repeatedly lose out to urgent requests. These can also be signs of [marketing orchestration debt](https://www.heinzmarketing.com/blog/why-orchestration-debt-belongs-in-your-2026-marketing-plan/), when inefficient workflows and disconnected processes create additional drag on the team.

The clearest sign may be that everyone agrees on what needs to happen, but no one can confidently say when it will get done.

To understand what is getting in the way, look at the work that has already been approved but has not moved forward. Does it lack a clear owner? Is the owner responsible for too many other priorities? Is the work waiting on expertise the team does not have? Or does it require coordination across so many people that no one has enough time to drive it?

Those are different problems, and they require different solutions. Lack of clarity may call for strategic guidance. Broken handoffs may require a better workflow and a more [orchestrated approach to how marketing work gets done](https://www.heinzmarketing.com/blog/gtm-vs-marketing-orchestration/). A missing skill may require specialized expertise. But when capable people know what to do and simply cannot absorb more work, you likely have a marketing team capacity issue.

## Be honest about what the team can support

Marketing organizations are being asked to do more on nearly every front. They need to create demand, support sales, improve the customer experience, adopt AI, produce more content, and prove the revenue impact of it all. At the same time, many are operating with flat budgets and lean teams.

That makes prioritization essential, but prioritization cannot solve every capacity problem. There is a point at which everything remaining on the list is legitimately important. Choosing among those priorities may reduce the pressure temporarily, but it can also mean accepting the cost of delaying work tied directly to growth.

Leaders need a realistic view of what each initiative requires, who is available to do it, and what those people already own. A campaign may appear to be one item on a roadmap, but launching it could require audience research, messaging, content, creative, marketing automation, paid media, sales enablement, reporting, and project management. If the plan accounts for the campaign but not the people and time required to produce it, it is not yet executable. A [well-rounded campaign plan](https://www.heinzmarketing.com/blog/how-to-create-a-well-rounded-campaign-plan/) must account for all of those activities, along with the people and time required to complete them.

## Decide what kind of help you actually need

Not every capacity gap requires a permanent hire. In fact, hiring may not be the best answer when the need is immediate, tied to a specific initiative, or likely to change in a few months.

Sometimes a team needs temporary staff augmentation: an experienced person who can step in, work as part of the team, and keep campaigns moving while an employee is out or a role remains open. Sometimes the team has execution resources but needs a consultant to establish the strategy, priorities, or operating model. In many cases, the right solution combines both. The team needs help improving how the work gets done while also needing someone to help do it.

The important thing is to be specific about the gap. “ *We need marketing help* ” is too broad. Do you need someone to make decisions, manage the work, perform the work, or build the team’s ability to handle it independently in the future? Clear answers will help you find the right kind of support and avoid paying for a strategic engagement when what you urgently need is execution.

## Do not wait until the team is underwater

Capacity issues become more expensive when they are ignored. Delayed campaigns mean delayed learning, delayed pipeline, and sometimes missed market opportunities. The strongest employees often compensate by taking on more, which may keep things moving for a while but is difficult to sustain. Better marketing orchestration can improve execution while also [supporting a healthier team culture](https://www.heinzmarketing.com/blog/can-marketing-orchestration-improve-team-culture/). Eventually, quality drops, important details get missed, or good people burn out.

The better time to address capacity is when you can first see that committed work and available resources no longer match. That does not mean immediately adding headcount or outside support. It means making a deliberate choice: stop something, delay something, simplify the work, reassign it, or bring in help.

The question is not whether your team is working hard enough. In most cases, they are. The more useful question is whether the organization has given them a realistic way to deliver what has been promised.

If your strategy is sound but your most important campaigns are still sitting on the roadmap, another planning session may not be what you need. You may simply need more hands, the right expertise, and someone with the time to turn the plan into work that reaches the market.
