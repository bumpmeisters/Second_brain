## Executive Summary

The lead-based demand generation model was not a victim of poor execution. It is simply out of date — designed for a world that only ever existed for a brief moment in time, and which has long-since disappeared altogether.

That world ran on information asymmetry: buyers needed sellers to learn about products, which made inbound contact a reliable signal of purchase intent. The internet ended that asymmetry two decades ago. What kept the lead model in place ever since was not evidence of its fit. It was institutional memory.

Data from more than 10,000 B2B buying journeys makes the mismatch impossible to ignore. Buying groups of 10 or more people evaluate silently and asynchronously, rarely filling out forms. Four of five vendors are shortlisted on Day One, before research begins. Buyers select a preferred vendor before ever engaging with sales roughly 80% of the time — and purchase from their original shortlist 95% of the time. Sellers are not guiding the journey. They are being invited to validate decisions that have already been made.

Meanwhile, lead-based systems misread the professionally curious as in-market buyers, manufacture friction between marketing and sales, and measure form fills while real buying groups go unseen.

This manuscript presents the evidence, explains the structural shift, and offers a practical path forward — from the metrics to abandon, to the operational model required to compete in the world buying actually happens in.

The document is intended to help you make the case for moving on from leads in your organization.

## Why the Lead-Based Model Collapsed

For more than two decades, the lead-based model has formed the heart of B2B go-to-market practice — determining how marketers allocate budget, how sales teams prospect, how success is measured, and ultimately how organizations value marketing. It has persisted despite the fact that the most widely adopted framework for managing leads, the [SiriusDecisions Demand Waterfall](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/meetthenewestsiriusdecisionsdemandwaterfall/), shifted from leads to buying groups back in 2017.

To be clear, the lead-based approach was never the most appropriate model for B2B. It may have been the only one most teams could operationalize given the technology available at the time, but it was never the best fit. The reasons are straightforward:

- At any given time, roughly 60% of target accounts are not in-market. B2B professionals consume category-relevant content continuously, which means most visitors to vendor websites are curious professionals, not active buyers.
- B2B purchases are made by groups of 5 to 20 or more individuals. The most reliable signal of in-market activity is not one person showing interest, it’s multiple people from the same account showing interest at the same time. One person is noise. Two is a pattern. Each additional signal increases the probability that an organization is genuinely in a buying journey.
- High-consideration B2B purchases are not triggered by clever emails, ads, or calling campaigns. Organizations enter market when business conditions create the need or opportunity. Those moments can be observed but rarely caused.
- As content marketing matures, vendors build audiences of professionals who value their thinking. This is good for brand, but it systematically inflates the pool of non-buyers filling forms and engaging with content, making lead volume an increasingly unreliable signal.
- The lead model implicitly assumes marketing’s job is to deliver qualified prospects to sales. In reality, modern B2B revenue requires four distinct jobs: 1) expanding category participation, 2) building brand preference, 3) enabling buying groups to reach consensus, and 4) capturing late-cycle demand. Lead-centric systems over-rotate on the last of these while starving the other three.

The consequences are predictable: BDR teams chase the professionally curious rather than genuine buyers; marketing is measured on contact names while sales is judged on opportunity conversion; and measurement systems reward visible activity — form fills, MQLs, rather than meaningful progress toward revenue; friction between the two functions becomes structural rather than situational.

Lead capture mechanisms have a role to play, but only as part of a broader buying group identification strategy. As the primary demand signal, leads are far more likely to obscure in-market buyers than to reveal them.

## Why Leads Were Once Worth Chasing: The Role of Information Asymmetry

The lead-based approach was developed for an era in which sellers controlled access to product information. Just 20 years ago – early in the careers of most of the senior leaders in B2B revenue organizations today – buyers still had to engage with sellers at the beginning of their journeys. Company website content was not yet robust enough to support nascent buying journeys, and buyers had not yet become accustomed to doing their own research.

When buyers had few options for learning about vendor offerings apart from speaking with sellers, a lead was a reliable early indicator of genuine interest.

B2B organizations developed a hunger for those leads. The method that seemed to work to attract them was to have gated website content. Organizations built content marketing functions to populate those websites and attract the potential buyers. Marketing automation platforms came along to help collect email address from form-fillers.

But as organizations got better at publishing content potential buyers were interested in, the nature of those content consumers changed in a way that is, with the hindsight available to us today, perfectly predictable. More and more people visited B2B websites to consume content, but not because they were in-market. Instead, they were simply interested in the content.

In addition to vendor websites, countless independent sites sprang up to give practitioners content they wanted and needed to do their jobs well. The internet and content marketing flattened that asymmetry nearly two decades ago. It has been virtually flat ever since. Buyers have had access to a wide variety of information sources — including their own accumulated experience from prior buying cycles — and rarely begin their journeys by contacting sellers.

The lead model didn’t fail because marketers operated it poorly. It failed because it was designed for a world that no longer exists.

## What Holds the Old Model in Place

What keeps the old model in place is not evidence; rather, what has stalled progress in B2B revenue production practices are the deeply embedded systems, processes, misattributions, and institutional narratives — what we call the [MQL-Industrial Complex](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/what-mql-industrial-complex-kerry-cunningham-trwhc/). A key element of the MQL-Industrial Complex comprises the stories organizations have developed — their success narratives. These narratives emerged organically as companies launched and scaled and serve multiple purposes. They celebrate the heroes and heroic actions that built and scaled to company. And they shape the culture, the personality of the companies.

In many organizations, these explanations hardened into unquestioned narratives of how success is achieved – not just in the past, but in the present and future, as well. Unfortunately, many of those narratives simply do not match the realities of current market conditions.

Ultimately, these misplaced narratives produced what we call [folklore debt](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/folklore-debt/): inherited beliefs that persist in the stories a company tells itself about how success happens long after the conditions that made them true have disappeared.

## The Truth About Modern B2B Buying

Today’s reality looks nothing like the information-asymmetric world that spawned the folklore that holds the lead-based processes in place.

Modern buyers are groups, not individuals. They populate shortlists from personal experience at the outset of their journeys, expect ungated access to information, evaluate silently and asynchronously, and identify a preferred vendor before engaging with sellers more than 90% of the time. Understanding how they actually do this is where any serious reform of B2B revenue practice has to begin.

On average, 10 to 11 individuals participate in a B2B solution evaluation, each bringing their own experiences, biases, and preferences. Directors read analyst material and comparison guides. VPs review case studies and financial outcomes. Champions dig into documentation and integration details. Procurement validates risk. The C-suite validates strategic fit. Each contributes dozens of interactions and digital footprints.

The process is not a rational journey of discovery. It is non-linear, often disputatious, and shaped as much by prior opinion as by new information. As many as [40% of buying journeys](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report) collapse because the group cannot reach consensus. In the [2024](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/2024-buyer-experience-report/) and [2025](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/) Buyer Experience studies, we observed:

- 16 to 17 interactions per person per vendor
- Fewer than 30% of eventual buyers ever filled out a form on the winning vendor’s website
- Vendors see only 10% to 15% of total research activity

Yet most B2B organizations still rely on tools built for an asymmetric world in which the buyer is believed to be an individual with autonomous purchasing power. MAPs treat individuals as proxies for opportunities. CRMs can’t surface buying group activity. BDR processes ignore multi-threading. Reporting prioritizes lead counts over opportunity intelligence.

## Your Buyers Are Experienced, And That Changes Everything

The most important pattern in the 2025 buyer experience report data is also the most underappreciated: buyers do not begin with a [blank slate](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyers-are-even-less-of-a-blank-slate-than-we-thought/).

Buying group memory — the accumulated professional experience distributed across group members — is the dominant force shaping early decisions in established solution categories. Before a journey begins, buying group members have participated in an average of more than eight partial or complete buying processes in relevant categories. This includes what might appear to be secondary personas: financial ratifiers, technical evaluators, and procurement representatives report just as much category experience as primary buyers.

Every member of the buying group arrives with opinions about which vendors are credible, enterprise-ready, safe, or innovative – and opinions about which of these factors should be weighted more heavily in the selection process. These opinions are formed over years — from past jobs, tools used, analyst quadrants skimmed, and conversations had. Buying groups carry their own narratives about vendors into every new evaluation, and organizational folklore about vendors that “always work” or “never work” travels with business leaders across companies and careers.

The early buying journey is therefore not primarily about discovery. It is the refinement and discussion of pre-existing beliefs and experience. From day one, buying group members are advocating for their favorites. Vendors are being ranked before the first piece of research is consumed.

Competitive advantage is built as much in the years before a buying journey begins as in anything that happens during it.

## The Selection:Validation Buying Journey Framework

Our research has revealed a consistent pattern in how B2B buying groups identify and evaluate, then select and validate providers.

### The Day-One Shortlist & Pre-Loaded Preference

Perhaps the most consequential finding in the [2025 Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/) is the structure and importance of the day-one shortlist.

Across nearly 4,000 buyer journeys studied in 2025:

- Buyers typically evaluate five vendors.
- Four of those five are placed on the shortlist on day one of the buying journey, before any research begins.
- Buyers purchase from this list 95% of the time.
- Buyers have prior experience with nearly every vendor on the list (97% of buying group members from all roles are familiar with *at least* one vendor on the shortlist).

Our research has found that once initial shortlists have been populated, B2B buying proceeds in two phases, Selection and Validation:

- **Selection phase** refers to the period of time during which a buying group begins pursuing solutions to a recognized need and uses accumulated experience and brand memory to filter the market, align internally, and narrow toward a preferred vendor.
- **Validation phase** begins once a consensus has emerged within the buying group about the ordering of the shortlist. At that point, the team turns to vendor conversations to validate assumptions and facts that informed preliminary consensus and culminates with deal negotiations.

In the first two years of our research, we found that the Selection Phase occupied the first 70% of the buying journey. But our most recent collection in 2025 surfaced a structural shift in buyer behavior.

Today, the journey is closer to 60% Selection, 40% Validation.

Importantly, this shift to earlier engagement does not mean buyers are now less likely to pick a winner before engaging with sales. Instead, the Selection phase – including buying group consensus on a preferred vendor – has been compressed into less time.

![](https://6sense.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Lead-Based-Approach_Visual-1.png)

The work required to select a vendor — comparisons, shortlisting, early feasibility checks, alignment on requirements, product education — now happens faster for two reasons.

### Economic Uncertainty Forces Earlier Cross-Functional Alignment

Buyers are under pressure to perform, have earmarked budget for solutions that they think will help them achieve their goals, and are concerned that their budgets may evaporate before the desired solutions can be acquired. They are in a hurry to get evaluations done and solutions acquired.

### “AI Inside” Requires Earlier Validation

Modern solutions increasingly embed AI at the core of their architecture. Our research shows that 89% of solutions purchased by our participants had an AI component. Even in heavy equipment and machinery categories, 67% of purchases included AI features. But to acquire these AI-enabled solutions, buyers must validate data lineage, governance, integration pathways, model transparency, and usage safeguards far earlier in the cycle.

And while buyers are experienced in relevant categories, this is a set of checkboxes they have not had to tick in prior evaluations. To handle these additional evaluation chores while still moving at an accelerated pace, buyers are engaging with sellers earlier.

In contrast, LLMs are not driving earlier first contact, nor is the use of LLMs leading to shorter buying cycles. Ninety-four percent of our participants reported using LLMs during their journey, but those who did not engaged sellers earlier, not later, than those who did. And, buyers who used LLMs were more likely to use them toward the middle of their journeys than at the beginnings, when buyers are often assumed to be “discovering” vendor options.

**What “AI Inside” Means for the Buying Journey**

The phrase “AI Inside” refers to the growing expectation — and reality — that business solutions embed artificial intelligence. Our 2025 Buyer Experience research found that 89% of solutions purchased included an AI component, a figure that reached 67% even in categories as traditionally hardware-oriented as heavy equipment and machinery.

This changes the buying process in a specific and consequential way: AI-enabled solutions require earlier cross-functional validation than buyers have required before. Unlike prior evaluation cycles, where buyers largely understood the information security and legal compliance implications of a solution category, AI Inside presents new challenges for buyers.

Specifically, buying groups must now evaluate:  
• Data lineage and provenance — where does the model’s training data come from, and who owns it?  
• Governance and accountability — who is responsible for AI-generated outputs?  
• Integration pathways — how does the AI layer interact with existing data infrastructure?  
• Model transparency — can the vendor explain how the AI reaches its conclusions?  
• Usage safeguards — what controls exist to prevent misuse or unintended outputs?

In many categories, these are not questions experienced buyers have answered before, nor are these questions adequately addressed in available online content.

As a result, a period of greater information asymmetry has arrived, requiring buyers to engage directly sooner. Buyers are pulling technical, legal, and procurement stakeholders into the buying group earlier and engaging with sellers sooner, specifically to validate AI-specific risks. And yet, before they do, they are still arriving at consensus on a preferred vendor.

Implication for go-to-market teams: vendors that arrive in the Validation Phase without AI governance documentation, model transparency resources, and integration architecture guides will lose deals they should have won. ### The Surprising Impact of Economic Uncertainty

Contrary to popular wisdom, when business leaders have budget established to solve a challenge or address an opportunity, economic uncertainty often compresses, rather than extends buying cycles.

### The Net Effect of Economic Uncertainty and AI Inside

Buyers reach the point of first contact earlier, but not because LLMs are speeding their initial research.

They reach consensus earlier because they are under pressure and anxious to start the evaluation of “AI Inside.”

Sellers still enter after preference has solidified.

### Airtight Alignment at the Selection: Validation Border

The goal of the Selection Phase for a buying group is to align on a consensus vendor, and that alignment is airtight by the time buyers engage with sellers. When asked why the buying group might switch away from their Selection Phase winner once Validation begins, only 2.6% of buyers said that stakeholder pushback could derail the process. Said another way, by the time buyers reach consensus, they are very certain that all stakeholders are in firm agreement about why the shortlist has been ordered as it has — and equally certain about what would cause the group to change its choice.

By the time the buying group enters the Validation Phase:

- The shortlist is ordered by preference (94% of buyers said they ordered their shortlist according to preference before engaging sellers).
	- The late-cycle battle is between closely-ranked finalists, not the entire field.
- Terms that could reshuffle the shortlist have been established before vendor conversations begin.
- Shortlist displacement is rare (occurring in about 2 out of 10 purchases), requiring overwhelming evidence.

The implication is clear: B2B companies must compete over the long arc of preference development — not only during active buying cycles, and certainly not only near the end of the buying cycle, in what we think of as the Validation Phase.

Brand equity, mental availability, sustained presence, and consistent buyer enablement are not “nice-to-haves.” They are required for survival.

### The Rules of Validation

The single most important force impacting a buying group as it reaches consensus and enters the Validation Phase of the buying journey is confirmation bias. On average, buyers have spent six months coming to consensus. At this point, their focus shifts to completing validation with minimal friction.

Once buyers enter the Validation Phase, the job of go-to-market teams fundamentally changes. This is no longer a period for repositioning or reframing the problem. Buyers are not seeking new ideas; they are seeking reassurance. New ideas may impact how buyers view future purchases, but they are unlikely to shift how they decide this one.

#### Done and (Nearly) Dusted

Ninety-four percent of buyers have picked a favorite before the start of the Validation Phase, and that favorite wins about 77% of the time. Still, 77% is not one hundred percent. A substantial number of deals will be won by a vendor that was not in first place to start the Validation Phase.

Being in the lead at the Validation Phase is clearly a massive advantage. Knowing if you are in the lead will require asking the right question of the right person in the right way. Fortunately, buyers are overwhelmingly likely to set up their first formal vendor conversation with the vendor that is in the top slot. Simply asking the primary buying group contact, “Are we the first vendor you are meeting with?” will provide a reliable read on whether you are in the top slot. If the answer is yes, you very likely are.

#### What Can and Cannot Be Done Once Validation Begins

Without question, buyers’ primary goal during the Validation Phase is to reduce decision risk. They arrive already knowing what they want to confirm: that their preferred vendor can deliver what they believe it can, that pricing can be resolved within acceptable range, that the features they’re counting on hold up under scrutiny. This is not a discovery process. It is a verification process.

Providers in the pole position should act accordingly. That means establishing the relative importance of decision criteria early, meeting each criterion with credible customer evidence, reinforcing financial and operational stability, and equipping buying group members with the materials they need to defend the decision internally.

Marketing’s role is to support inevitability.

Sales’ role is not to persuade but to validate — to confirm the buyer’s existing beliefs and resolve lingering doubts. Vendors that understand this to shorten cycles, preserve trust, and dramatically reduce late-stage derailment. Those that don’t may lose deals they had already won.

**For vendors that are not the top choice entering the Validation Phase, t** he most common mistake is behaving as if the decision criteria are still at issue. They rarely are. By the time buyers enter the Validation Phase, they have established both a preferred vendor and the specific conditions that would cause them to change that choice. Late-stage attempts to introduce new value narratives, surface additional use cases, or reposition the solution are unlikely to move the buying group **off their initial choice.**

By clearly understanding and effectively speaking to established buying criteria at the point of first contact with buyers, selling teams give themselves the best chance of moving up in the rankings. Challengers are tempted to try to shift the decision criteria at this stage. That may be the only play available to sellers, but doing so rarely changes a buying group’s choice.

At the same time, vendors that enter the Validation Phase as underdogs can meaningfully influence their future prospects. Only 23% of buyers change their vendor choice during the Validation Phase, but 42% say their preference shifted. A non-winning vendor can gain ground for the next cycle. Overturning six months of consensus is a different matter entirely.

#### Exceptions to the Rule

From time to time, entirely new categories emerge. At any given time, roughly a quarter of buying journeys are exploring these new categories. In the past, these have included the transition from on-premise infrastructure to cloud computing, the rise of marketing automation, and the emergence of customer data platforms as a distinct software class. In these moments, buyers genuinely lack prior experience and must educate themselves. But these situations are the exception, not the norm — and they do not describe how most B2B buying decisions are made most of the time.

#### Advice: Don’t Build for the Exception

When buyers genuinely lack prior experience — most often because a category itself is new — the rules do change. In these cases, education, category framing, and early thought leadership matter far more than they do in mature markets. But this is precisely why such situations require different go-to-market approaches, not generalized ones.

The mistake many organizations make is designing their entire demand strategy as if they are perpetually educating a new audience about the category. In reality, most B2B companies compete in established categories where buyer experience is deep, preferences are pre-loaded, and shortlists form immediately. Treating these environments as educational problems leads to over-investment in content meant to educate the educated instead of sustained investment in brand memory and preference formation.

## AI, LLMs, and the Evolving State of Buying Journeys

LLMs have reshaped how professionals stay informed — but not in the ways many expected. While common wisdom maintains that buyers no longer visit provider websites during their journeys, the data tell a different, more nuanced story.

Recent buying-group members report roughly the same number of vendor interactions as in prior years (16 per person per vendor). At the same time, many B2B vendors report meaningful declines in overall web traffic.

### The Casual Information Consumer vs. the Active Buyer

Our research consistently shows that at any given time, the majority of a B2B company’s target audience is not in-market. Even so, professionals in those out-of-market accounts routinely monitor developments in the solution categories that affect their roles. Historically, this ambient information consumption generated substantial vendor website traffic.

LLMs have disrupted that pattern. They have quickly become the default tool for low-stakes learning in support of category update scanning and vendor capability updates. Activities that once drove exploratory website visits now often result in LLM queries instead.

This shift primarily affects **out-of-market buyers**, whose goal is simply to maintain an updated mental model of the vendor landscape. With little at stake, they are more willing to rely on synthesized secondary sources.

However, when buyers enter a formal purchasing process, their behavior changes. With budget, and reputational risk on the line, buying-group members return to primary sources — including vendor websites — to validate claims, assess fit, and support internal justification.

This resolves the apparent contradiction in market signals:

- Overall website traffic is declining
- Engagement from in-market buyers remains stable
- Opportunity creation remains largely intact

In-market buyers have not abandoned vendor websites. The composition and intent of visitors have shifted.

### What LLMs Are Not Changing

LLMs are not materially altering core buying dynamics. They do not:

- Shorten buying cycles
- Accelerate first contact
- Increase vendor influence
- Reduce the need for buying-group alignment
- Change participation breadth
- Replace in-market vendor evaluation

Our statistical models attribute less than 2% of variability in these outcomes to LLM usage.

When organizations are truly in market, buying behavior looks strikingly similar to pre-LLM patterns:

- hundreds of buying group interactions per vendor
- multi-source information validation
- heavy documentation review
- cross-functional evaluation
- anonymous research across many sites
- continued reliance on trusted advisors (76% of buyers used external help in 2025 vs. 71% previously)

What LLMs add is a powerful synthesis layer that helps buyers process large volumes of information on the path to consensus.

**In short:** LLMs are changing how buyers maintain category awareness when out of market — not how they evaluate vendors once in market.

### Strategic Implication: Interpreting Traffic Loss

The danger is misdiagnosing what traffic declines actually mean.

If the lost visits are primarily low-intent monitoring behavior, attempts to “win them back” through heavier gating, more aggressive retargeting, or traffic-centric KPIs risk damaging trust without improving pipeline outcomes.

In the LLM era, the objective is no longer to maximize raw traffic. It is to ensure:

- Mental availability when buyers are out of market
- Decision confidence when buying groups enter the Selection Phase

Organizations that conflate traffic loss with demand loss risk optimizing for activity that does little to increase the number — or preference — of real buying groups.

### The Danger in the LLM Traffic Loss: Day 1 Shortlist Positioning

The real danger of losing traffic to LLMs is not losing the attention of buyers once they are in-market — it is losing their attention prior to when they enter the market. We know that buyers establish their shortlists at the outset of their buying journeys. Prior to the outset of the journey, it may be that buyers have not recently visited vendor websites. Their mental models of vendor offerings – their preferences and biases — may be largely a product of their prior experience and what they have read through LLMs since the last evaluation cycle in the category. As such, these buyers may not have been exposed to a brand’s latest positioning, portfolio updates and branding.

Organizations that are not investing in maintaining their presence in the minds of future buyers run the risk of missing out on Day 1 shortlists or simply starting lower on the list than they should have.

## Buying Group Intelligence: The Only Reliable Demand Signal

B2B buyers, typically groups comprising 5–20 individuals. An in-market buying group may have hundreds of interactions across a wide variety of media and channels in the course of their buying journey. That content consumption is very unlikely to follow pre-established content sequences developed by marketers. Instead, buying group members engage in disordered, largely anonymous content consumption across digital properties, and set requirements before speaking with sellers.

At the same time, leads — the default demand signal — may come from individuals at any stage of a buying journey. They are equally likely to come from the countless individuals who have a professional interest in the solutions and content vendors provide, but who are not part of an in-market buying group.

### Context is King: Overcoming the Foam Fallacy

Marketing organizations must pique and satisfy the curiosity of potential future buyers. Marketers must both build compelling content and experiences to draw out-of-market buyers to engage with their owned and operated properties and events. At the same time, digital content must be made available and credible for human and LLM consumption.

Less than half of in-market buyers will fill in a form to view vendor content. Thus, when content is only accessible after form-fill, consumption of that content drops dramatically. Both humans and the bots that feed the LLMs will fail to be influenced by content they cannot or will not fill in a form to see. Even when individuals are enticed to fill in forms, the result will primarily be an ever-increasing volume of leads from high-fit-but-low-intent individuals that are not in market yet and which do not convert.

What B2B organizations need in order to capitalize on buyer content consumption is context. Revenue teams must be able to distinguish the in-market from the professionally curious. By focusing on signals from in-market buying groups, organizations can reduce false positives and decrease false negatives (missed buying journeys), while still building affinity with future buyers.

Contextual intelligence integrates:

- Anonymous website traffic
- Multi-individual patterns
- Third-party intent
- Topic-level behavioral signals
- Volume and velocity indicators
- Cross-signal coherence
- Buying stage inference

This is the only reliable method for identifying:

- Which accounts are in market
- What stage they are in
- Whether intent is individual curiosity or collective action
- Which opportunities are truly real
- How to prioritize seller attention

Leads miss nearly all of this reality.

This is the shift from:

- Individual → Collective
- Ambiguous → Interpretable
- Reactive → Predictive
- Lead → Opportunity

## Rebuilding Revenue Production Around Buyer Reality

Moving beyond a lead-based model is not primarily a matter of adding new tools or running new campaigns. It requires a series of conscious tradeoffs — decisions to stop optimizing for behaviors that no longer map to how buyers buy, and to start optimizing for signals that do. Most lead-era metrics overweight demand capture while under-counting the work that leads to sustained growth and competitive success. The table below summarizes the most common tradeoffs revenue teams must make when aligning to how buyers actually buy.

| Stop Optimizing For… | Start Optimizing For… |
| --- | --- |
| Lead volume and conversion rates | Opportunity creation and progression |
| Individual buyer activity | Account-level and buying group engagement |
| Form fills as intent signals | Collective buying signals and behavior patterns |
| Speed to first contact | Relevance to buying stage |
| Content as a capture mechanism | Content as decision infrastructure |
| Funnel stages imposed on buyers | Selection and Validation as distinct phases |
| Marketing-sourced leads | Marketing-influenced opportunities |
| Late-stage persuasion | Early preference and late-stage risk reduction |
| Traffic growth as success | Mental availability and shortlist inclusion |
| Attribution models | Lift, progression, and opportunity impact |

These shifts are not cosmetic. They change how budget is allocated, how teams are measured, and how success is defined. Organizations that make them consistently generate higher-quality pipeline, waste less seller effort, and reduce the friction buyers experience when moving toward consensus.

The question for revenue leaders is not whether these changes are coming, but how long they will continue optimizing for a system that no longer reflects buyer reality.

## The Role of Intent Data

Intent data is the most polarizing technology in B2B. The fact that some hate it while others love it is less a function of the ‘quality’ of the data and more a function of how it has been used.

Used properly, what we think of as intent data works. It identifies account-level activity revenue teams would otherwise miss, and flags active buying groups, often just as they’re forming. The promise was real, and the technology has delivered on it.

But for many, it seemed like a pure flop. And when it seems to flop, the first thing people say is “the data is bad.”

That’s not quite right. What’s usually missing isn’t better data — it’s understanding what the signals really mean, and how to respond to the signals effectively.

Understanding begins here: Our research consistently shows that roughly 60% of a well-defined ICP isn’t in-market at any given time. Of the remaining 40%, about three-quarters are in early journey stages — months from buying or even from taking a meeting. *These are high-fit accounts actively assessing their options, but unlikely to take your call.*

The reality is that most buying journeys end after several months of research and debate without making a purchase (Nuancing the 95/5). As a result, the vast majority of buyers that are in-market at any given moment are early in their journeys. Send BDRs after them with the sole purpose of booking meetings, and those attempts fail most of the time. Worse, the BDR break-up email lands while the buyer is still weighing your merits against competitors. You chalk up another “intent data failure” — while the buyer is still evaluating you.

The signals aren’t wrong. They are the best signal we have of a buying group that is in the process of evaluating vendors and choosing a favorite. This is where future pipeline and revenue will come from. But, what 10,000 buyers have told us is that the typical member of such a buying group does not want to talk to sellers during this early period.

Your efforts to reach them are likely to fail, not because the signals are wrong, but because we are misinterpreting them. Most intent data isn’t a buyer saying pitch me.

But, it is something just as valuable: a buying group in the early stages of building preference, when they’re most persuadable. Treat it as a sales trigger and you get burned. Treat it as a signal to provide that buying group with your very best information and experiences to help build preference for your solutions and you get results.

Same data, different framework for understanding and taking action.

## Measurement in the Post-Lead World

Measurement is the final barrier to modernization. Lead-based metrics distort performance. They reward the wrong behaviors, obscure the true drivers of revenue, and deny marketers credit for the work that produces opportunities.

### Modern Measurement Focuses On:

- Opportunity creation
- Buying stage progression
- Volume of individuals engaged per account
- Anonymous + known engagement patterns
- Lift analysis (not attribution)
- Opportunity influence (not lead sourcing)
- In-market conversion rates

This shift fosters alignment across marketing, sales, and RevOps.

It also fundamentally improves financial performance, as evidenced by organizations that tie marketing compensation to opportunity creation rather than leads — reporting an 8% improvement in outcomes even when lead volume decreases.

### Measurement to Shed and What to Replace it With

| What Must Be Retired | Why It No Longer Serves Us | Replace It With |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lead Volume | Counts individual identities captured | Buying group engagement |
| Lead Source Attribution (first/last touch) | Incorrectly assigns causality to proximate actions, ignoring how buyers buy | Opportunity lift analysis — what increases the likelihood of opportunities developing and progressing |
| Marketing-sourced pipeline | “Sourced” metrics fail to account for the long tail of influences that lead to pipeline and revenue | Opportunity influence / lift |
| Cost per lead | Cost per lead is irrelevant to revenue production | Buying group contacts engaged |
| Lead-to-opportunity conversion | Lead conversion rates are only distantly and weakly related to pipeline and revenue production | Opportunity engagement and progression |
| Contact-level engagement | Ignores anonymous behavior and obscures coordinated buying signals | Anonymous + known engagement patterns; key buyer persona engagement |
| Lead Conversion | Not associated with revenue production | ICP conversion rates |

**A Note for RevOps Leaders**

Revenue Operations teams sit at the center of this transition. Historically, RevOps has been tasked with creating order in a chaotic go-to-market system: standardizing definitions, enforcing process discipline, and producing metrics executives could trust. In a lead-based world, that often meant optimizing for what could be easily counted, even when those counts were imperfect – or very poor – proxies for progress toward revenue. Revenue operations is where success narratives become hardened folklore debt.

The risk now is not that RevOps will resist change, but that it will inadvertently preserve outdated assumptions by continuing to operationalize lead-era metrics with greater precision. Improving the accuracy of the wrong measures does not improve outcomes — it entrenches them. The greatest risk is not resistance to change, but the continued industrialization of yesterday’s assumptions.

In a buying group–driven world, the job is no longer simply reporting on activity, but to surface evidence of meaningful progress: which accounts are in market, where they are in their journey, how effectively does the organization gain admittance to short lists, and where deals are at risk of stalling or derailing.

This shift requires RevOps leaders to prioritize buying-stage inference, buying group-level progression, and opportunity realism over attribution just-so stories and conversion rates. It also requires partnering closely with marketing and sales to ensure that measurement reveals buyer reality rather than wishful thinking and internal process comfort.

Done well, this evolution elevates RevOps from an enforcement and accounting function to a strategic one. RevOps becomes the connective tissue between buyer behavior and revenue strategy — not the custodian of a system that no longer reflects how decisions are actually made.

## Beyond Demand Capture: How Category and Brand Maturity Inform GTM Strategy

So far, we have seen that buyers are experienced, and that they form shortlists before they engage. For companies that occupy commanding positions in the categories in which they compete, this amounts to a competitive advantage. For everyone else, particularly occupants of densely competitive categories, the fact that buyers largely establish the competitors that will be allowed in the race for their business before formally entering the Selection Phase means that your most important competitive window occurs before the journey starts. That naturally raises the question: *So what do you do about it?*

Optimizing revenue production is not simply a matter of moving on from a leads-based approach. B2B teams need to align to the full reality of how buyers buy, which includes the go-to-market (GTM) context in which your company operates.

In the section that follows, we describe how GTM context should influence the role of brand and category marketing.

### ‘Must-have’ or ‘Nice-to-have’ Frames How You Should Compete

Not all solution categories are mandatory purchases. Some — payroll, cybersecurity, CRM — are effectively universal. Every organization of meaningful size eventually buys something in the category. Others are discretionary: valuable, often compelling, but not purchased by every account that could theoretically benefit.

This distinction matters enormously,

Providers operating in must-have categories need not worry about whether the category is healthy. Where a category is necessary to operate the business, competitors within the category can compete against each other without being concerned about confusing buyers and limiting category growth.

In non-mandatory categories, competing mainly for share by taking deals from rivals is often a negative-sum game. When the number of active buyers is fixed or growing slowly, aggressive differentiation does not expand the market; it simply reallocates demand within a pie that is not getting bigger. Every dollar one vendor gains comes at another’s expense (negative sum article). Worse, when providers attack competitors’ approaches or question what the category can actually deliver, buyers can become uncertain about the category itself. That uncertainty can stall growth or even shrink the market, as cautious buyers become less willing to commit.

In these categories, growing the number of accounts that participate in the category — convincing the unconvinced that the problem is real and the investment is justified — is not a branding luxury. It is a survival mechanism. Organizations that skip this work and compete only for active demand are, in effect, betting that someone else will do the category-building while they reap the benefit. That bet sometimes pays. It more often results in fighting harder for a stubbornly resistant pool of buyers.

The first question, then, is this: what share of your ICP is realistically in-market in any given year, and what would it take to increase that share? If the answer involves growing category participation — not just winning a bigger slice of existing demand — then a substantive portion of your budget and resources need to be devoted to building the brand of and demand for the category, not simply your own brand.

### Category Maturity and Brand Presence Shape GTM Strategy

Once you have established whether the category is mandatory or optional, there are two further considerations:

**Category Maturity** (horizontal axis): How established is the solution space? Mature categories have clear business cases, defined evaluation criteria, recognized vendors, and high participation rates. In mature categories, buyers are typically experienced, even expert in buying and using the solution. Emerging categories require buyers to be educated about the problem before they can evaluate solutions. In emerging categories, buyers often rely on analysts and consultants for guidance on whether and how to inter the category.

**Brand Presence** (vertical axis): Given the nature of your category, your position within it is the second variable that shapes which moves are available to you.

How well known and trusted are you among your ICP accounts? High brand presence means approaching saturation-level awareness — buyers know you, have opinions about you, and are likely to include you on a Day One shortlist. Low brand presence means you are still building recognition and earning consideration – and probably missing out on a lot of potential deals that you never have the opportunity to contest.

### Identifying Your Competitive Niche: The Category-Brand Matrix

![](https://6sense.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Lead-Based-Approach_Visual-2-1.png)

The Category-Brand Matrix maps that position across two dimensions: how established the category is, and how recognized your brand is within it. These two dimensions produce four distinct competitive positions, each with its own strategic logic:

**The Pioneer** (Low Brand, Emerging Category): You are building the category and your brand simultaneously. Category education is urgent — there is no pie to compete for until buyers understand why they need a solution. Competitive differentiation is premature and potentially damaging: multiple vendors each promoting different category narratives fragment buyer understanding at the moment when clarity is most valuable.

**The Challenger** (Low Brand, Established Category): The category exists and buyers are purchasing — but not from you. Your primary challenge is breaking into shortlists that form without you on them. Differentiation matters here, but feature competition against entrenched players is unlikely to win. Reframing what the category is for, who it’s best suited to, or what good looks like is often more powerful than claiming to do the same thing better.

**The Explorer** (High Brand, Emerging Category): You have brand credibility but are entering territory that isn’t yet defined. Your trust assets allow you to accelerate category definition — buyers will take the category narrative more seriously coming from a brand they already respect. The risk is using that credibility to extend existing product lines rather than genuinely defining a new space.

**The Leader** (High Brand, Established Category): You are on virtually every shortlist, and your brand is identified with the category itself. The primary job is maintaining that position while managing commoditization pressure and competitive threats from challengers attempting to reframe the category around criteria you don’t dominate.

For a full treatment of the Category-Brand Matrix and its strategic implications, see *[Winning in the Brand-to-Demand Zone](https://steinb2b.com/perspectives/brand-to-demand-zone-playbook)*.

### The Four — Jobs and Why the Balance Differs by Position

![](https://6sense.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Lead-Based-Approach_Visual-3.png)

Modern B2B revenue comprises four distinct jobs. The lead-based model over-rotates on only one of them.

- **Job 1 — Competitive Slice**: Win active evaluations. Capture in-market demand from accounts already in a buying journey. This is the job the lead-based model was designed for and the one most GTM systems are built around. This model works best in mandatory and highly mature solution categories.
- **Job 2 — Journey Survival**: Help buying groups reach consensus. An estimated 40% of buying journeys that begin and persist for months or quarters never result in a purchase. The blocking factor for this enormous segment of potential buyers is not budget or need recognition, but simply that the buying group cannot align internally. Enabling the groups that are evaluating you to complete a decision keeps the pie from which your competitive slice is taken from shrinking. This job is underinvested by most organizations.
- **Job 3 — Category Size**: Journey survival is about nurturing the journeys of buying groups that have recognized a need that your solution could address. The Category Size job is about increasing the number of organizations that come to this realization. Buyers must recognize when their business needs align to the affordances provided by your category of solution. This is the job that grows the size of the pie, before it can be maintained and distributed.
- **Job 4 — Category Flavor**: Shape how the category is understood and evaluated. Define the criteria that matter, the language that describes the problem, and the frame through which buyers assess competing solutions. Is the category about cost savings, business growth? Efficiency or productivity? Flavor work is what makes your differentiation powerful and shapes how buyers position requests for budget and resources.

The right balance across these four jobs differs by position on the matrix:

- **Pioneers** need to over-index on Jobs 3 and 4 before Job 1 is even viable. There are not enough deals in motion to optimize for competitive slice. Organizations that default to Job 1 in emerging categories often damage the category by introducing competitive noise at the moment when buyer clarity is most fragile.
- **Challengers** need Jobs 2 and 4 most urgently. Getting on shortlists requires reframing the category (Job 4). Winning deals once you’re there requires helping the buying group reach consensus (Job 2). Job 3 is largely wasted — the category already exists and is established. Job 1 can only have an impact when buyers have accepted your category reframing as potentially valid.
- **Explorers** need Jobs 3 and 4 first, using brand credibility to accelerate category construction before competitive dynamics take hold.
- **Leaders** are the one position where Job 1 demands first billing, although not to the exclusion of Job 4, which is what prevents challengers from successfully reframing the category around criteria you don’t own.

## Operational Transformation: Crawl → Walk → Run

### Why the Maturity Assessment That Follows Focuses on Job 1

Having just argued that over-rotation on competitive demand capture is the central failure of the lead-based model, it is fair to ask why the framework that follows focuses primarily on that job.

The answer is not that Job 1 is ultimately the most important. It isn’t – or at least it shouldn’t be. But the reality for most B2B organizations today is that most marketing efforts are targeted at Job 1, and failure to demonstrate competence and growth here will almost certainly prevent marketing leadership from being able to refocus the necessary attention on Jobs 2 through 4.

Also, the data, signals, and many of the organizational capabilities required to execute Jobs 2, 3, and 4 are built on the same foundation as Job 1: knowing who your ICP is, identifying which accounts are in market, understanding where buying groups are in their journeys, and aligning marketing and sales around account-level behavior rather than individual lead activity. You cannot sustain investment in category growth or buying group enablement without the operational discipline that competitive execution demands.

The maturity assessment that follows is therefore a diagnostic for the foundation — the prerequisite to everything else, not the ceiling of ambition. Organizations that score well on it are not done. They have earned the right to invest more deliberately in the jobs that grow the pie, not just their share of it.

With that context, the assessment:

- Stage-specific opportunity prioritization — spending resources proportionately to the likelihood of winning.
- Blended marketing and selling tactics — aligned buyer programs designed around the buying journey rather than internal function transfers.

The transformation away from leads requires operational sequencing.

## In Closing: A System Was Built for a Different Time

The lead-based model did not fail because the people who built it were wrong. It failed because the conditions it was designed for no longer exist.

It was built for a world in which sellers controlled access to information, buyers had limited experience evaluating complex solutions, and the act of requesting information from a vendor was a meaningful signal of purchase intent. For a time, those assumptions held. They do not hold today.

Buyers now arrive at the table already knowing the category, already familiar with the vendors, and already carrying preferences formed through years of professional experience. They have, on average, been through more than eight buying journeys in the categories relevant to their roles. They form shortlists before they begin formal evaluation. They reach consensus before they engage with sellers. They arrive in the Validation Phase already having made their choice.

In this environment, a system built to capture the interest of unknown buyers and convert them through persuasion is not just inefficient — it is invisible to the buyers who matter most. The buying groups organizations most want to reach are the ones least likely to announce themselves through a form fill.

### What Changes — and What Doesn’t

Moving beyond the lead-based model does not mean abandoning discipline. It means redirecting it.

The organizations that will produce sustainable revenue growth are the ones that invest in being known, trusted, and preferred long before a buying group enters the market. They build brand presence that is wide enough to reach out-of-market buyers and specific enough to mean something when those buyers arrive at a buying decision. They identify in-market accounts through buying group behavior, not individual contact capture. They equip sellers to work the Validation Phase — which means supporting conviction rather than attempting persuasion.

And they measure success not by the volume of leads the system produces, but by the quality and progression of the opportunities it reveals.

### The Maturity Gap is Real — and Closable

Most B2B organizations are not starting from zero. They have data, technology, and talent that can be redirected toward the reality of B2B buying. The GTM Maturity Self-Assessment in the preceding section is designed to help revenue leaders identify where they are on that journey — and where the most consequential gaps lie.

The Crawl stage is not a failure state. It is an honest starting point for organizations building toward a system that reflects how buyers actually buy. What matters is not where the score lands today, but whether the organization is moving in the right direction — from counting individuals to understanding groups, from reacting to inbound signals to anticipating buying behavior, from optimizing for lead volume to optimizing for opportunity quality.That movement, sustained over time, is what separates the revenue teams that will thrive in the next decade from those that will continue wondering why their lead programs produce so little pipeline.

### A Final Word on Institutional Memory

The hardest part of this transition is not technological. It is cultural.

Lead-based thinking is embedded in the vocabulary, the processes, the incentive structures of and the organizational folklore of nearly every B2B revenue organization. It is the language executives use when they ask marketing for “more top-of-funnel.” It is the metric RevOps reports when the board asks about pipeline coverage. It is the KPI that determines whether a marketing team gets credit for a quarter’s results. It is the story executives tell the troops to rally them to bring home a big quarter.

Sloughing off this MQL-industrial Complex requires more than buying new technology or running a different kind of campaign. It requires naming the assumptions embedded in the existing system, explaining why they no longer hold, and building the evidence base for a different set of assumptions. That is the work this report is intended to support.

The lead-based model has enjoyed an extended stay at the heart of B2B revenue production. But the buying scenario it was built to identify has moved on. It is time for revenue teams to move with them.
