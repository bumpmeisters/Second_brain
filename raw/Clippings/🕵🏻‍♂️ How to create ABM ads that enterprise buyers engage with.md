## Targeting before the creativity

Before the creative part, I have to share the fundamental rule: targeting.

We recommend picking one specific use case, then building a cluster of accounts around it.

Then decompose the use case. Write down the core challenges your buyers face, the root reasons behind them, and the primary buyer persona that faces these challenges. This decomposition is the raw material for every ad you’ll create.

**I wrote a full breakdown of use-case-based targeting here:** [https://fullfunnel.substack.com/p/how-to-target-accounts-in-2026](https://fullfunnel.substack.com/p/how-to-target-accounts-in-2026)

## The three layers of ads

Once you have your cluster, you’re working across three layers.

- **One-to-many:** ads that cover typical challenges of the whole use-case cluster and are relevant to all accounts regardless of their buyer journey stage.
- **One-to-few:** pick a specific role and speak to one specific challenge or goal. Good for accounts already in-market for a solution.
- **One-to-one:** personalized ads for a specific account and its buyers. This is my favorite and the most creative layer.

Let’s break them down one by one.

## One-to-many

My favorite frameworks for this layer come from my friend Shlomo Genchin. Here is the process he uses, plus one actionable example we created live for Apicbase.

Most B2B ads are literal and feature-centric, or broad and generic. They are wrapped in generic stock photos or UI screenshots that look like any other ad. It’s a safe play because this is what is perceived as “professional” in B2B marketing. The common fear is that B2B buyers (not from marketing and sales space) will penalize creativity and see it as unprofessional.

So they just vanish in the feed of the sameness.

Shlomo’s framework does the opposite. It connects the product’s features to the buyer’s actual day-to-day frustrations and delivers them with a visual you can relate to in one second.

Here is step-by-step process how to create ABM ads that grab attention and engage buyers.

### Step 1: Run dual-path research

You gather two categories of data.

- The technical path: the product’s actual features, capabilities, and specs.
- The human path: the relatable situations, quotes, and everyday work frustrations. The fastest and the easiest way is to search Google, TikTok, or Reddit for industry memes (”HR memes,” “software engineering memes”) to pick up the exact humor and vocabulary of your audience.

Here is how we applied it to [Apicbase](https://get.apicbase.com/), the AI-native operating system for multi-site food-service businesses.

The technical path is recipe, menu, and inventory management across locations.

The human path is restaurant managers exhausted by endless disconnected spreadsheets, drowning in food waste, over-ordering, and lost margins.

Anke Artois from Apicbase told us:

> *Multi-site operators waste a lot of stock because they don’t really know what they have on hand. Counts are off, what’s in the system doesn’t match what’s on the shelf, and the result is over-ordering, spoilage, and real margin loss every week. That’s what our customers care about most. One bad review stings for a day. A persistent margin leak across dozens of sites costs them real money every month. We’re here to solve the second one.*

### Step 2: Define the core conflict

Every ad needs tension. Use one of two models.

- World without: ask what is the worst possible thing that can happen if people don’t use this product, then exaggerate it for storytelling effect.
- The promise vs the problem: define the core promise of your product, then build the message around its exact opposite.

For Apicbase, the over-ordering problem is the promise of clean inventory contrasted with the nightmare of food spoiling in the fridge.

### Step 3: Capture the “that moment when” insight

Write your core pain points starting with the phrase “that moment when.” It’s the same setup stand-up comedians use to make an audience recognize a shared truth instantly.

> For Apicbase: *“That moment when you over-ordered again, and have a full stock of spoiled food.”*

This captures the regular frustration of their buyers.

### Step 4: Create a visual hook

Simplify the story so the audience understands it in the first second.

- **Before vs after:** a side-by-side of best case and worst case. For Apicbase, the left side shows a full stock of food “Before Apicbase,” the right side shows a clean empty fridge labeled “After Apicbase”
- **Analogy ads**: if your product is abstract, find a physical analogy from another universe (ill-fitting clothes to visualize ill-fitting enterprise software).
- **Art over stock photos:** take a standard image and use generative AI to turn it into high-contrast, vivid still-life photography. For Apicbase, an artistic shot of a fridge stuffed with rotting, spoiled food hooks managers fighting over-ordering and inventory problems weekly.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!65ET!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa9c852f5-e7d4-49e9-a81f-8d3419a7e8de_1598x852.png)

### Step 5: Apply creative copywriting techniques

Here are a few ideas.

- **Flipped phrases:** take a recognizable idiom and swap one word (”one size doesn’t fit all” for custom software development companies).
- **Human traits:** “ *This jacket is a climate denier.”*

We ended up with the next headline: *“Your margins get spoiled the same way as your food.”*

### Step 6: Create multiple options, then filter them

Creativity is a numbers game. Fill a Figma board with dozens of rough layouts, images, and headline variations. Expect to throw away 90% of your drafts. The goal is to filter the volume down to the 5 to 10 strongest, strategically aligned ideas.

## One-to-few examples

I like how UserGems run their one-to-few ads. They personalize with the logo of a specific account plus recognizable logos as social proof, aimed at companies already doing ABM and outbound across scattered tools, and position themselves as the one platform that unifies it with an AI layer.

Here is how Trinity Nguyen, CMO of UserGems, described her playbook during the keynote at our Full-Funnel summit:

> *“Most marketers stop advertising once an opportunity is created. They think it’s the AE’s territory, and marketing steps back. We don’t. Our team is measured by revenue alongside the sales team, so we keep helping deals to the finish line. Not many companies run this play yet.*
> 
> *The mechanism is multi-threading, getting as many people from the buying group into the conversation as possible. We’ve measured this over years: when an opportunity is multi-threaded, we get a 5X higher win rate than when it’s single-threaded.*
> 
> *Here’s how we run it:*
> 
> 1. *Define your key personas. For us it’s three: sales leaders, marketing leaders, and sometimes rev ops leaders.*
> 2. *Trigger the ads on opportunity creation. When an opp hits stage one or two, we automatically pull those accounts into an audience in our ad campaigns and start running ads to them.*
> 3. *Keep the ask soft. The message is basically, you’re already talking to us, do you want to join the conversation? We don’t expect them to click.*
> 
> *The real job of these ads isn’t the click. It’s helping the AE multi-thread. It’s a delicate thing for an AE to ask a champion, “can you bring in your sales leader?” The ads do that work for them.”*

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qHPj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc3dae459-e1cb-4155-8173-5a672386d7f3_1200x732.png)

The part I’d underline: when an opportunity is multi-threaded, they see a 5X higher win rate than when it’s single-threaded. Most marketers stop advertising the moment the baton passes to sales. Trinity’s team keeps running soft-ask ads at the full buying group precisely at that stage, because they’re measured on revenue with sales, not on leads handed over.

## One-to-one examples

Here is the most creative ABM ad I’ve ever seen. It’s what inspired me to create the “account love letters” framework.

10 years ago I read an article about Andrew Medal and a tactic he used to create awareness among strategic accounts. Andrew was running a marketing agency targeting enterprise accounts, and the only way to grab their attention was to stand out. Here is what he did.

1. For every strategic account he was targeting, he wrote an article for Forbes or Huffington Post (back when those outlets generated massive awareness). The entire article was about how that account could grow, mentioning the tactics, programs, and channels where Andrew’s agency had real expertise. If I were doing this, I’d write something like *“10 ways Influ2 can generate enterprise deals with ABM.”*
2. When the article is published, Andrew and his team post about it on social, tagging the key executives. Then they reach out sharing the link and suggesting a brainstorm session on how to implement the ideas. If they don’t hear back, Andrew moves to the next step.
3. Andrew creates a banner with the headshot of the CMO he wants to meet, adds the text *“Hey! Do you know John Doe? Tell him I need to get in touch with him,”* and a link to a landing page. Then he runs that ad to all the key execs plus the target department. As you’d guess, it’s pretty unignorable.

## But our buyers are conservative

I hear this objection a lot. Here is the question one attendee of Full-Funnel Live asked me: *“Our procurement committee has CFOs who haven’t changed their system in a decade. If I run a creative ad at them, I’ll look like I don’t understand their world. Where’s the line between creative that grabs attention and creative that kills credibility?”*

Before the 1950s, B2C ads were as boring as B2B is now. You sold a car by showing the engine, because buying a car was serious business. Then Bill Bernbach ran creative ads for Volkswagen, “Lemon” and “Think small,” and people started loving them. B2B works the same way. If you can be creative selling a $100,000 car, you can be creative selling $200,000 software.

The trick is understanding their world well enough that the joke lands on real pain, instead of clowning for the sake of it. A VP scrolls memes on the toilet like everyone else.

A few years ago we ran an ABM campaign with a client selling hedge risk mitigation software for European manufacturers producing outside the Eurozone, where currency swings were killing their export margins. The visual was a rollercoaster. We ran it for 3 months. Then every engaged account got a direct mail parcel with a Lego rollercoaster and the same line: your margins shouldn’t be on a rollercoaster every day.

Not everyone replied. But the ones who did mention it on the discovery calls, smiling, months later.

So a conservative audience will happily accept the joke, as long as it’s based on something they actually feel. We are all humans.

## The most common mistake and how to avoid it

Trying to be creative and funny and not being funny.

“Fun” doesn’t mean comedy. It means slightly entertaining, slightly unusual. Something that stands out in a feed full of AI slop and relates to the daily headaches of the target buyer persona.

Lean into what your team is actually good at. Great copywriters? Do copy. Subtle, intelligent humor? Do that. Don’t copy the company doing skits if that’s not you.

Define your own way to be creative because it’s one of the things that AI can’t do yet (and, probably, will never be able) to.
