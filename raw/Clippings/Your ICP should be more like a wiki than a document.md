# Your ICP should be more like a wiki than a document

We feel better when things are documented and published. Consistent, tangible, official. But sometimes fluid is better.

Messier? Yep! But when your market and customers are changing this fast, you need to embrace fluidity instead of fighting it.

I’m thinking a lot lately about ideal customer profiles relative to this.

Fluidity is not an excuse for full-on chaos, i.e. not defining your ICP tightly to begin with. But how confident are you that what currently exists as your documented ICP is still right, right now?

## The “perfect” ICP that slowly gathers dust (and irrelevancy)

Building an ICP is too often a one-time exercise. You get sales, marketing and maybe product into a room (or more likely a shared document) and you work backward from your best customers. Revenue. Industry. Headcount. Technology stack. Decision-maker titles. You write it down, share it at the next all-hands and put it in a folder.

Fourteen months later, maybe your GTM teams are still creating content and targeting accounts based on it. Maybe.

Nobody updated it after the three biggest accounts in your legacy top-tier segment churned. Nobody updated it when sales started winning deals in a vertical that wasn’t in the profile. Nobody updated it when product shipped a capability that opened a completely new use case with a completely different buyer.

The ICP document that helped you focus initially slowly became a compliance artifact.

The market doesn’t agree that your ICP is finished. Your best customers keep evolving. Your product keeps changing. The companies you sell to keep hiring new people into new roles who make decisions differently than the person you modeled the profile around.

The ICP that closed your best deals a couple years ago is likely missing something that closes your best deals today.

## Who SHOULD you be selling to?

The most useful reframe I’ve found (and one we’ve used with clients at Heinz Marketing for years) is changing one word in the question.

Most ICP conversations are built around “who can we sell to?” That leads to lazy targeting. It expands the profile to include anyone who might conceivably buy, and you end up chasing a lot of business that looks right on paper and turns into a messy, sometimes way-too-public divorce.

The better question: who “should” we sell to?

That means the subset of the market that most acutely matches what your product actually delivers: where your go-to-market motion works, where your product team is building the capabilities those customers need and where the expansion motion after close looks as good as the initial deal.

Equally important, and almost never clearly enumerated: who should you stop chasing? There’s lots of bad business out there for all of us. Business that feels good when you ring the new-deal bell and ends badly. The best ICPs include disqualification criteria as clear as the qualification criteria. Most don’t.

## Three inputs that should be updating your ICP (and probably aren’t yet)

If the ICP is going to be a living artifact, something has to feed it. Most teams have at least three rich sources of truth they’re almost entirely ignoring.

**Lost deals, examined honestly.**

Not a CRM disposition code. Not “pricing” or “went with competitor” in a dropdown. A real debrief: What stage did it die? What was the stated reason versus the actual reason? What did the prospect say they were going to do instead? Did that tell you something about whether you were solving the right problem for the right customer?

Almost every loss interview surfaces something. Win-loss research consistently finds that a majority of lost-deal interviews reveal signals that could have changed how the deal was run, or whether it should have been pursued at all. Your pipeline is telling you who’s wrong for you. Most teams aren’t listening systematically.

**Churned customers, looked at honestly.**

This is the most uncomfortable exercise in B2B. The discipline is to look at the accounts that churned and ask not “why did they leave,” but “should we have taken this business in the first place?”

That question produces a different answer. “Why did they leave” produces blame. “Should we have taken this” produces ICP criteria.

If your top-line churn numbers look fine but certain segments churn at 40% while others churn at 10%, your ICP criteria aren’t discriminating what you think they’re discriminating. You’re capturing accounts that look right at signing and show you something different by month eight.

**Surprise wins, unpacked.**

The most underused signal in B2B is the deal that shouldn’t have been a great fit, but turned into one of your best customers. Most teams celebrate the win, log it in the CRM and move on.

The better question: what was true about that account that your ICP didn’t predict? Was it a use case you hadn’t mapped? A buying trigger you hadn’t noticed: a leadership change, a competitive displacement, a funding event that created urgency? A team structure that made your motion land differently than expected?

Those anomalies are your ICP’s next version. They’re telling you where the profile needs to grow.

## What a living ICP practice actually looks like

This doesn’t require a new tool. It requires a cadence and an owner.

**Monthly.** After every closed-won and closed-lost deal of any significance: a structured 10-minute debrief captured somewhere the marketing and CS teams can actually read. Not a pipeline note. A flag: did this account fit our ICP criteria on paper? Did it feel right or wrong in practice? What did we learn? These become the raw material for the quarterly conversation.

**Quarterly.** A 60–90 minute cross-functional review with sales, marketing and CS (and ideally a product voice in the room). Input: the monthly flags, cohort churn data for the quarter and a look at any deals that felt like exceptions. Output: specific, named changes to ICP criteria. This is an editing session, not a philosophical conversation. You’re adding, removing or sharpening the criteria based on what the last 90 days taught you.

Losses tell you more than wins, especially when you’ve built a system that captures the patterns. What did product miss? What did marketing fail to surface? What did sales hear but never elevate? Treated as a learning system rather than a blame audit, this is some of the most valuable time your GTM team can spend each quarter.

**Annually.** A full rebuild from the top 20% of customers by retention and expansion. Who are they, really? What do they have in common that your current ICP doesn’t capture? This is the reset that prevents incremental drift from compounding over years into a profile that’s fundamentally off from where your best growth is actually happening.

**Ownership.** Most ICPs have no owner. Marketing thinks sales owns it. Sales thinks marketing does. The fix is a named person. Sometimes a product marketer, sometimes a head of growth, in some cases the CMO directly. That person isn’t writing the ICP alone but is accountable for making sure it gets updated by the people closest to the signal. When nobody owns it, it drifts. Or worse, gets ignored.

AI is making all of this more achievable than it used to be. The pattern-recognition work that once took a RevOps analyst a week now runs faster and with more data: synthesizing win/loss data, flagging cohort churn differences, identifying segments where deal velocity is breaking down. The right AI workflow feeds that conversation with better-analyzed input so the time in the room is spent on decisions, not assembly and argument.

Product capabilities (from you and others in your market) are evolving faster than most sales and marketing motions can absorb them. Marketing is often generating leads against an ICP definition that was maybe right two years ago. Get the teams in the room more frequently, with better data feeding the conversation.

## More bad news (and the pragmatic path through it)

Here’s what makes this even more difficult: updating your ICP means accepting that some of what’s in your current pipeline doesn’t belong there. That’s uncomfortable. It means disqualifying accounts sales has been working, acknowledging that a segment leadership endorsed at the last offsite isn’t producing and admitting the last version of the profile maybe needs to change.

This is why I like the “wiki” analogy for ICPs now. A wiki is expected to be updated. A wiki edit means the artifact is working the way it’s supposed to. Nobody treats it as an admission of failure. A document, by contrast, is expected to be right when published. The form itself implies permanence. Changes feel like failure instead of progress.

Changing the mental model from document to wiki also shifts the organizational dynamic. The quarterly update is maintenance. The person who flags that the ICP criteria needs to change isn’t undermining the work that came before. They’re doing the work.

Practically, start with the data you already have. Pull your top 20 closed-won accounts from the last 12 months. Score them against your current ICP criteria. If more than a third of your best customers don’t actually fit your stated ICP, your document is already behind, and you now have the conversation-starter you need to move it forward.
