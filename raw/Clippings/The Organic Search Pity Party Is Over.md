# The Human Element of Community Cannot Be Automated (So I Let AI Do The Rest)

> Quelle: https://mattheinz.substack.com/p/the-human-element-of-community-cannot
> Veröffentlicht: 2026-06-13T17:33:11+02:00
> Geclippt am: 2026-08-21

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3yD6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb02df4f1-aaf8-4f21-b360-4dbbec7e8342_4284x5712.jpeg)

CMO Coffee Talk members in line at Franklin’s BBQ in Austin during our third-annual “CMO Meat-Up Weekend”, March 2026

Running a healthy, vibrant community is an honor, and what you see above the surface is truly the fun stuff.

The conversations, both in live sessions and 24/7 in Slack. The connections someone makes that turns into a job, or a vendor switch, or a friendship. With CMO Coffee Talk in particular, Friday mornings are when nearly four hundred marketing leaders show up to learn, share, cry. That’s on top of almost 4,000 CMOs in a very active Slack channel.

Those channels and interactions are amazing, and it’s why I keep doing it. But that part is maybe ten percent of the actual work.

The other ninety percent is finding the right people to invite, ensuring the right people are in, getting them in the door and making new members feel welcome, showing up in their feed, proactively engaging them with each other, pulling the best ideas out of a chat window before they scroll into oblivion and making sure the person who shared something brilliant on Tuesday gets a little credit by Friday.

In other words, running a community is a lot of little things every day, every week.

None of it is hard or rocket science. Almost all of it is repetitive, tactical and a little tedious. Which is exactly why most community organizers either burn out doing it or quietly stop doing it at all.

It’s also exactly the kind of work AI is good at now. So over the past year I’ve handed most of it off, grouped into the three jobs that foundationally help keep a community alive.

*Quick Disclaimer: Behind the scenes Taylor Berg at 6sense does the Lord’s work managing the membership database and complex Zoom set-up required for a group of this size. I consider that “back of house” work, the automations here are focused on “front of house” community management.*

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!D85Z!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc14f6eb9-365a-4762-874f-a8875391707b_2280x1080.jpeg)

**Finding and welcoming the right people**

A peer community only stays valuable if the right people keep joining. For CMO Coffee Talk that means actual CMOs and senior heads of marketing, and there are more qualifying potential members every week than any one person can track manually.

So a few things run on their own. Every Thursday, a prospecting agent pulls net-new CMOs from my Sales Navigator saved searches, cross-references them against what’s already in Salesforce and my running backlog, and adds only the genuinely qualified new names to the invite list.

Every weekday morning, another agent takes that backlog and builds the day’s outreach file so I’m working a steady, manageable number instead of a scary pile. And every weekday I get a scan of fresh CMO appointment announcements, with anyone already in the community flagged first so I can congratulate them before the news gets stale.

The welcome experience matters just as much as the invite. When a new member registers, an onboarding agent handles the unglamorous chain that used to eat so much time: update the record in Salesforce, drop them into the welcome sequence, add them to the right Sales Navigator list, follow them back. New members don’t need to see any of this. They just (hopefully) get a great first impression for a community that seems to have its act together.

This is all based on a strategic experience we want CMOs (members and prospective community members) to have. But based on a set of rules and workflows that are largely consistent (with improvements over time), it can be automated with occasional oversight.

More gets done, consistently and successfully, with my time focused on authentic, 1:1 interactions.

**Staying in front of members already there**

Most communities lose members not in a dramatic exit but in a slow fade. Someone joins, nobody ever engages with them, and six months later they couldn’t tell you they were a member. Staying visible to the people who already raised their hand is the least glamorous work there is, and the easiest to let slide.

A few agents make sure I don’t. Every morning I get a list of community members who posted on LinkedIn in the last twenty-four hours, with direct links, so engaging is a two-minute job instead of an hour of scrolling. A separate digest surfaces wins and content worth amplifying from our Slack and LinkedIn community page, so the people doing interesting things get noticed.

And the actual relationship-building steps I commit to — the connection requests, the follow-up messages, the long backlog of “I should really DM that person” — get pulled from my sequences and teed up so they actually happen instead of living permanently on a someday list.

We also now have an automated monthly CMO-to-CMO matching sequence in Slack that helps facilitate 1:1 interactions between members.

I’ve long had these workflows and ideas/features defined, they have just historically been manual and very time-consuming – which means sometimes they get done and too often they don’t.

But they’re critical to a healthy community. Early and regular engagement matters. A member who hears from you in their first month behaves completely differently than one who joined and heard nothing. And that continues to matter when someone notices that the community notices them, what they’re doing and achieving, etc.

**Capturing and repurposing what the community already knows**

The best and worst thing about a room full of smart marketers is that the smartest stuff often gets said out loud (great!) and then immediately lost in the flow (bad!).

A killer recommendation or insight shared in the Zoom chat at 9:40 on a Friday and by 9:42 it’s already hard to find. Multiply that across a year and you’re literally hiding the most valuable thing the community produces (it’s collective knowledge).

I sometimes describe the CMO Coffee Talk Slack channel (which includes more than six years of archived content, templates, best practices, etc.) as the greatest CMO library in the world but all the books are on the floor.

So we’ve built a series of habits that now run automatically each week to help members get the most value (including on-demand and searchable access) from everything that exists for them and from them.

For example, every Friday, the chat transcript from that morning’s session gets turned into a clean best-practices summary — the actual tools, tactics and recommendations people shared, organized and saved instead of evaporating. And it’s automatically added to a searchable folder with all past session highlights as well.

Every weekend, the week’s Slack activity becomes our community digest, so even members who missed everything can catch up in five minutes.

Once a month, any new resource someone dropped in Slack gets pulled into a running CMO Resource Library so good documents stop disappearing into the scroll. It’s literally an html file that links back to specific assets in Slack, all organized by topic and subject. Board slides, job descriptions, dashboard examples, everything.

And the best moments from the Friday chat get drafted into short posts I can share in Slack and LinkedIn, which sends a little attention back to the people who said the smart thing in the first place.

Capturing the community’s best ideas and reflecting them back is how members know their contribution mattered. It’s also the work I was really bad at doing consistently when I tried to do it by hand.

**The part I still (and will always) save for myself**

The point was never to do less of the work. It was to stop letting the tedious ninety percent of the work crowd out the ten percent that is the best use of my time.

The team of agents working behind the scenes now every day lets me spend more time engaging members directly. Connecting them with each other, creating new ways to drive value in the community, gathering feedback on new topics, curating great Friday topics and experts, etc.

A community is a trust account, and trust is built in the human moments. Automate and outsource the foundational but tedious background work. Spend as much of your time as possible on time WITH the community, not tasks for it.
