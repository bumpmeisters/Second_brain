# How to Come Back From Vacation Without the Panic

> Quelle: https://mattheinz.substack.com/p/how-to-come-back-from-vacation-without
> Veröffentlicht: 2026-06-29T01:28:29+02:00
> Geclippt am: 2026-08-21

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!d_Ij!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F444351a0-5356-4443-b64e-39214a9bfaf7_1080x675.jpeg)

On Tuesday afternoon I’ll close this laptop and head to San Diego (Encinitas more specifically #iykyk) with my family. I won’t touch this laptop again until Monday, July 20th.

I used to dread that. Not the vacation, of course. The day AFTER the vacation.

We do this trip every year now. And if that Monday morning is like the others, I’ll come back to thousands of emails, hundreds of Slack messages and dozens of Slack DMs waiting for me.

Ten years ago that backlog would have eaten my first three days and most of my good mood. Last year, I was down to about 35 emails by noon, Slack was back under control, and I spent the afternoon on a couple priorities I already knew about plus a couple that had come up while I was gone and genuinely needed me.

The difference wasn’t discipline on vacation. I was as checked out as ever, and that’s the point. Our team knows I’m dead serious about PTO — you’ve earned that time and we don’t want to hear from you!

The difference last year, the reason why I didn’t have a pit in my stomach the night before re-entry, was a process I continue to hone.

You deserve your PTO. And everyone should be able to do both halves of it: actually shut it down without guilt, then come back with a plan that gets you your bearings fast instead of drowning you on day one.

Here’s the system. Half of it happens before you leave.

### Before you leave

**Get to Task Zero, not inbox zero.** Your inbox will refill no matter what you do, so chasing it to zero the night before is a waste of an evening you should spend packing. The thing that actually matters is getting your real priorities and dependencies done and off your plate, so they’re off your mind and supporting other people’s work while you’re gone. A clean inbox feels good for about an hour, while finished dependencies earn you two weeks of quiet.

**Delegate in advance, and be specific about it.** Think through what would normally land on your plate over the next two weeks and make sure someone has it. Not “keep an eye on things.” Name the thing, name the person, make sure they know. Then do this enthusiastically for your teammates when it’s their turn. The whole thing only works if everyone covers for each other without keeping score.

**Write an out-of-office message that actually closes the loop.** Tell people definitively you won’t reply until you’re back. No “I’ll be checking periodically,” because then you’ve signed up to check periodically, and you’ll resent it by day three of your time off. Redirect anyone with something urgent to the person who can actually help them while you’re out. A good OOO protects your vacation and theirs. Here’s what mine will look like starting Tuesday afternoon.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Kzk-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa0c6da17-98c6-416d-855c-5e31589c522e_1520x1580.png)

**Block your first day back before you leave.** If you can, block the entire first day to get your bearings. Worst case, block the morning, or even just a few hours. Put it on the calendar now, before someone else fills it with a 9am meeting you’ll walk into jet-lagged and unprepared. Bonus points if you also schedule your re-entry briefing now (more on that below).

### When you’re back

**Delete aggressively.** Ninety percent of the backlog either isn’t important, has already been handled while you were out, or will circle back to you on its own if it’s truly important. So treat the backlog as guilty until proven innocent. Scan, delete, move on. The stuff that needs you has a way of finding you again.

**Triage the rest into “need now” and “catch up later.”** Of the ten percent left, most can wait. Move those into a separate folder you’ll get to over your first week. What’s left after that is a small handful that will feel much more manageable, and that small handful is where you can actually spend time and energy on your actual first day back. Suddenly thousands of messages are down to maybe a couple dozen things that matter and a folder you’ll chip at over coffee.

**Get a briefing.** Sit down with your team or your manager and get the real download on what you missed. Fifteen minutes of someone telling you what actually happened beats two hours of you reverse-engineering it from email threads. It saves them time too, because they get to point you at what’s urgent instead of watching you rediscover it slowly. Schedule it before you leave so it’s waiting for you.

**Give yourself grace.** If you’ve been gone a week or two and you truly left work behind, your brain needs time to find its rhythm again. You will be wiped out by mid-afternoon of your first day. That’s not a sign you’re behind. It means you actually rested, which was the whole idea. Let yourself be tired and trust that the rhythm comes back by day two or three.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HVlk!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffbedab57-a1f2-4ba5-824c-164d022d878e_2080x1080.png)

None of this is complicated. It works because it’s simple and straightforward. It’s also hard because you probably, like me, have to change some mindsets and habits to make it work.

Find a version of this that works for you but preserves your hard-earned time away and respects the people and priorities around you, before and after.

Give yourself that same gift the next time it’s your turn to disappear for a while.
