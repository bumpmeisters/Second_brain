Most B2B revenue teams aren’t failing because of bad execution. They’re failing because they’re executing well against assumptions that stopped being true a long time ago.

Data from more than [10,000 B2B buying journeys](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/the-leadbased-approach-has-failed-us-its-time-to-move-on/) reveals a consistent pattern: **The mental models that govern how most teams allocate budget, measure success, and deploy sellers are systematically misaligned with how buyers actually buy.**

The gap between assumption and reality is the reason pipeline forecasts disappoint, BDR teams burn out, and marketing and sales spend every quarter relitigating whose fault it was.

**Below is a distillation of the broken assumptions doing the most damage,** drawn from recently released analysis by Kerry Cunningham, a former SiriusDecisions and Forrester analyst, who has built the case in comprehensive detail on the [Science of B2B](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/) research hub. His full report, [The Lead-Based Approach Has Failed Us: It’s Time to Move On](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/the-leadbased-approach-has-failed-us-its-time-to-move-on/), is a 41-minute read and worth every minute.

## Myth #1: ‘A person who fills out a form is likely to buy.’

This is the foundational premise of lead-based demand generation, and it was never quite right. The model is simple enough: offer valuable content in exchange for contact details, then follow up with those contacts because they’re probably buyers. Except they usually aren’t.

[B2B purchases typically involve 10+ stakeholders](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/#summary-of-2023-and-2024-buyer-experience-study-findings). A single person downloading a whitepaper is most often a sign of professional curiosity, not purchase intent.

When a form fill is your primary demand signal, you get a lot of noise, a lot of leads that look bad on paper but might genuinely convert in 18 months, and a lot of BDR effort spent on people who were never going to take a meeting.

The better your content gets, the worse this problem becomes. You build a genuine audience, which is valuable for brand, and your revenue team spends the next quarter chasing practitioners who had no intention of buying anything.

## Myth #2: ‘Form-fill leads are the gold standard for predicting pipeline.’

The entire lead scoring apparatus rests on this assumption. Score enough behaviors and the leads at the top of the queue will represent real intent. Except: [Fewer than 30% of eventual buyers](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/the-leadbased-approach-has-failed-us-its-time-to-move-on/) ever fill out a form on the winning vendor’s website.

**The buying group members who shaped the decision never appear in the lead report:**

- The anonymous researchers doing early-stage due diligence
- The technical evaluators stress-testing integration requirements
- The VP who read three case studies and said nothing
- The procurement lead who vetted risk without ever visiting the website

A measurement system built on form fills isn’t measuring demand. It’s measuring the fraction of the buying group willing to trade an email address for content and treating everyone else as if they don’t exist.

## Myth #3: ‘Buyer education happens after a lead is generated.’

Your buyers are not blank slates waiting to learn from you. Buyers start their buying research with an average of eight or more prior buying journeys already behind them in the relevant category.

What follows isn’t passive absorption of vendor content. It’s months of independent research, internal debate, and stakeholder alignment, conducted largely without seller involvement.

**The most important educational work happens long before a form is filled out**. To influence this critical phase, you need to identify accounts in early buying motions and deliver content that helps buyers build the internal case for your solution, not just understand it.

Your champion needs materials they can circulate to financial ratifiers, technical evaluators, and skeptical executives. Buying group members who never contact you directly still form opinions that influence the final decision.

Most revenue teams underinvest here because early-stage buying activity is invisible to lead-based systems. If your measurement doesn’t identify opportunities until the form fill, you’re arriving to a conversation that’s mostly already over.

## Myth #4: ‘Sellers guide buyers to a decision.’

This assumption underlies most sales methodology, SDR sequencing, and pipeline stage logic. Sellers engage early, build relationships, shape requirements, and shepherd the opportunity to close.

[The 2025 Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/) finds that buyers reach consensus on a preferred vendor before engaging sellers the overwhelming majority of the time. Buying groups of 10 or more people evaluate silently, debate internally, and align on a shortlist before the first discovery call is scheduled.

By the time a seller enters the picture, the job isn’t to guide. It’s to confirm. That’s a fundamentally different motion, and most sales teams aren’t trained for it.

## Myth #5: ‘Intent signals are a trigger to call.’

When an account starts showing intent signals, the default response is to route it to a BDR and book a meeting. This produces consistently poor results and generates the “intent data doesn’t work” narrative that causes teams to abandon it.

The signals aren’t wrong; the interpretation is. Most buying groups showing intent are in the early selection phase, when they’re researching, debating internally, and building vendor preferences. They are not ready to take a sales call.

What those signals actually indicate is that a buying group is forming opinions about which vendors belong on their shortlist. The right response is to make sure that account encounters your best content, your strongest proof points, and your most compelling positioning. Treat intent as a trigger to influence, not a trigger to call.

## Myth #6: ‘More top-of-funnel means more pipeline.’

Call this the executive version of the lead volume problem. If roughly 60% of your target accounts aren’t in-market at any given time, accelerating contact volume against your full TAM mostly produces more professionally curious non-buyers. It inflates activity metrics, exhausts BDR capacity, and contributes nothing to pipeline.

Pipeline quality is a function of identifying which accounts are in a buying research motion and concentrating resources there.

## Myth #7: ‘Website traffic loss means demand loss.’

This is a newer assumption based on the impact of search engine AI summaries and direct research within LLM tools. Many revenue teams are watching website traffic decline and treating it as a leading indicator of weakening demand. The more accurate diagnosis: AI-generated summaries and LLM query tools have replaced the casual, low-intent browsing that out-of-market professionals used to do on vendor websites. Instead of clicking through to three blog posts to stay current on a category, they ask an AI and move on.

In-market buyers, the ones with budget and a real evaluation underway, are still showing up on vendor sites at roughly similar rates. The composition of visitors changed. Actual demand signals didn’t.

Teams that respond to the traffic decline by adding more gates or ramping retargeting spend are optimizing to win back an audience that wasn’t going to buy anyway.

## Myth #8: ‘Competitive differentiation is always the right play.’

Most GTM strategy defaults to differentiation: sharpen the messaging, tighten the positioning, make the case for why you’re better than the alternatives. In categories where every company of meaningful size eventually buys something, like CRM, payroll, or cybersecurity, this is largely the right instinct. Those deals are going to happen. The question is just who wins them.

In categories where purchase is optional, focusing too aggressively on differentiation can backfire by causing confusion and undermining confidence. When buyers see multiple vendors attacking each other’s approaches, some conclude the category itself is too risky or immature to invest in.

Aggressive competitive messaging in these markets doesn’t take share from rivals. It shrinks the pool of willing buyers for everyone. For companies in these categories, growing the number of accounts that see the problem as worth solving matters more than winning a larger slice of the accounts already in motion.

## Myth #9: ‘Our success proves our strategy works.’

The hardest assumption to dislodge, because it’s a cultural one, with company success lore often built on a foundation of incomplete data.

Every company builds success narratives: the campaigns that generated pipeline, the sequences that booked meetings, the product launch that drove a record quarter. Those stories are real. They’re also incomplete. Most measurement systems only capture what announces itself, and what announces itself is a fraction of actual buying activity.

What doesn’t get tracked is the buying group that evaluated the company anonymously and deselected them before a form fill; the account that was in-market, shortlisted two competitors, and closed without ever appearing in the CRM; and the deals lost before sales had any visibility into them.

Without visibility into those scenarios, companies that measure only what their current stack can observe will keep optimizing the visible slice, misallocating resources toward tactics that look productive, and wondering why overall results are harder to move.
