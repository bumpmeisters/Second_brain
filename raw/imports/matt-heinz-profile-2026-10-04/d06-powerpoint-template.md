## Summary

Why the best brand guidelines are installed rather than published, why a real designer matters more than ever and three ways to start. Brand consistency is shifting from a PDF nobody reads to a portable, plain-text skill that runs inside whatever AI tool an employee happens to use, with a human designer still building the system once and a review skill flagging issues before anything ships.

A friend last week told me that his company doesn’t bother maintaining corporate PowerPoint templates anymore.

Everyone has a skill installed instead. It’s managed through a plugin marketplace, so when he publishes an update, it gets pushed to every employee’s Claude. It also connects to ChatGPT, so anyone working there or in Codex gets the same results. His summary: “It’s very consistent.”

Is this the beginning of the end of the “frankenstein deck” that’s frustrated marketers and designers forever? I’d argue AI made that problem much worse initially, but the installed skill improvement could give everyone what they want.

## Brand guidelines were a PDF people largely ignored anyway

For as long as I’ve been in B2B marketing, brand consistency ran on the same three things: a guidelines document, a master template and a review step in front of anything important. It worked well enough when a small team made most of what shipped.

And honestly, it barely worked then. A benchmark study from Demand Metric and Lucidpress (a company that sells brand templating software) found almost every organization claimed to have brand guidelines, but only a quarter had formal guidelines they consistently enforced. More than 60 percent said materials still got created that didn’t conform to those guidelines at least sometimes.

Now everyone at your company is a designer, sort of. Sales, product, customer success, the CEO on a Sunday night. The guidelines PDF never stood a chance against that volume, and a part-time designer definitely doesn’t.

## Your brand has to work in every model in the building

The volume problem has a second layer, learned the hard way by another CMO I talked to this week. Her creative director got the brand dialed in inside one AI tool: one-pagers, web pages, slides, all on standard. Great work.

But a lot of the company used ChatGPT, and everyone also had Gemini through the company’s Google plan. When the creative director exported her setup as a markdown file and loaded it into ChatGPT, the results were (in her words) “not good.” The best brand work in the company was stranded inside one vendor.

You can mandate a single tool. But good luck with that. Microsoft found 78 percent of AI users at work are bringing their own AI tools. I bet that number will only grow in the next year. Your brand has to show up in whatever tool your people already use.

## Installed guidelines update themselves

What my friend built works differently. The rules, the template, the layouts and examples of good work live as a skill, a packaged set of instructions a model loads whenever it’s doing the relevant job. Nobody has to find the PDF, remember it or agree with it. It runs every time someone makes something. When the brand changes, it changes on every laptop at once.

The portability problem is also getting smaller. Anthropic recently opened the skills format as a standard, and OpenAI built the same structure into ChatGPT and Codex, with Microsoft adding it to VS Code and GitHub. That stranded markdown file is a lot closer to being something that travels.

This moves brand governance from a document you hope people read to software that runs whether they read anything or not. It also raises a question most brand teams have never had to answer: who gets to change the skill?

The answer will likely vary by organization. It could be the CMO, the head of brand or a designer. Either way, it’s one of numerous accountability decisions you should make internally and write down, the same way you’d build a RACI or DACI for any other shared process: who drives changes, who approves them, who gets consulted and who just needs to know. Decide it once and your team works faster and far more consistently.

## A real (human!) designer still builds the system

A person designs the system once, and the model applies it a thousand times.

For a team with finite design resources (and who doesn’t?), that changes where the team’s time should go. Building and maintaining the system every deck gets made from is worth far more of that time than fixing the next deck.

And the ceiling keeps rising. My friend with the installed template also ran a desktop agent that drove Illustrator and InDesign directly. His description: “Machine speed design. It was insane to watch it work.”

## Review can be a skill too

Another level? The deck polisher. It reviews every external deck before it goes out and flags fixes as critical, moderate or nice-to-have. It doesn’t make the changes, just recommends them, which I think is a good idea.

I wrote recently that [AI is making your best people slower Opens a new window](https://mattheinz.substack.com/p/ai-is-making-your-best-people-slower): every new asset lands on a senior reviewer whose day didn’t get any longer. A skill that does the first pass takes the obvious fixes off that reviewer’s plate and still leaves the judgement call with a real person.

None of this definitively rescues the frankenstein deck. But fresh work is easy to get on brand now. The old stuff living in sales shared drives and individual desktops is still a problem, and I’d argue the fastest fix for most of it is retirement rather than repair.

## Where to start

**Have a real designer build the system once.**

That might be someone on your own team or an outside shop. Either way, the job is a master template, dividers, a handful of layouts and examples of what good looks like. Budget the time or the money as infrastructure, because that’s what it is now.

**Write the brand down in plain text a model can read.**

The rules, the examples and the things you never do, in one file with one owner. Plain text travels between models far better than a setup tuned inside one tool.

**Install it centrally, then add a review skill that flags instead of fixes.**

Push it to every tool your people use (including the ones you didn’t pick), and let a polisher take the first pass on anything external before a human signs off.

Go pull up your current brand guidelines PDF. How much of it could be installed instead of published?

My guess is most of it, and whatever’s left is where your designer’s time should go.
