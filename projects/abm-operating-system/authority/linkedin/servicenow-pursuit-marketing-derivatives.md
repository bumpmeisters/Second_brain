## Post 1 — Strategy: a pursuit is not a sixth motion

**Artifact ID:** `linkedin-servicenow-01-strategy`

Pursuit Marketing is not a sixth ABM motion.

Treating it like one is how ABM operating models become catalogues of campaigns.

In a public interview, ServiceNow's Celia Slack described Pursuit Marketing as the sprint to ABM's marathon: concentrated account-based support around a material opportunity or challenge.

That delivery model is useful. But the strategic distinction matters more.

A Growth Motion answers: **where should the business sustain differentiated investment?**

A pursuit answers: **how should we intervene during this decision or risk window?**

The same pursuit format can therefore sit inside different motions:

- A strategic new-logo deal may need temporary 1:1 depth.
- A renewal or downsell risk belongs in Customer Expansion or recovery.
- An account without evidence, access, or sales commitment may need Account-based Demand before it deserves a pursuit.

Urgency changes the cadence. It does not decide the motion.

This is the first conclusion from my “Testing the Enterprise Growth System” series: keep the investment logic stable while allowing the intervention to move at sprint speed.

Otherwise every new format—deal-based, pursuit, executive engagement, event-based—demands its own box in the model.

The model gets bigger.

The decisions get worse.

**Speed belongs in execution. Strategy belongs in the motion.**

Source: public *Let's talk ABM* interview with Celia Slack. ServiceNow practices are source-reported; the Enterprise Growth System interpretation is mine. No endorsement is implied.

## Post 2 — Operator: collaboration is an admission gate

**Artifact ID:** `linkedin-servicenow-02-operator`

ABM teams should be allowed to say no to sales.

Not because alignment is unimportant.

Because alignment without contribution is not alignment.

ServiceNow's publicly described Pursuit Marketing practice uses three useful selection lenses: **opportunity, achievability, and collaboration**.

The third one is the operating breakthrough.

Before scarce ABM capacity enters a pursuit, I would require the account team to commit four things:

1. **Context** — what is actually happening in the account?
2. **Access** — how will we reach the people whose state must change?
3. **Decisions** — who can approve direction, risk, and follow-through?
4. **Action** — what will sales do after marketing creates progression?

Then add the question that intake forms usually avoid:

**What higher-value work will this pursuit displace?**

If the commercial owner cannot answer these questions, the request is not ready for high-touch ABM.

It may need intelligence work.

It may belong in Account-based Demand.

It may simply be an asset request.

But marketing should not compensate for missing ownership by accepting the work and hoping collaboration appears later.

That is how pursuit teams become internal agencies—and how hidden evening and weekend work becomes the scaling model.

The fastest way to make Pursuit Marketing scalable is to stop admitting work that was never a pursuit.

Source: public *Let's talk ABM* interview with Celia Slack. The four commitments and capacity question are my Enterprise Growth System adaptation, not ServiceNow's stated intake form.

## Post 3 — Critique: copy the governance, not the number

**Artifact ID:** `linkedin-servicenow-03-critique`

The most dangerous takeaway from ServiceNow's Pursuit Marketing story is the most memorable number.

The interview reported that one practitioner could cover roughly 20 pursuits per year, compared with roughly five accounts under durable 1:1 coverage.

That sounds like a productivity benchmark.

It is not one.

The source did not provide normalized workload, sprint length, account complexity, cohort definitions, audited outcomes, or a detailed staffing model. It also described a mature ABM environment with experienced practitioners, executive support, sales alignment, dashboards, CRM tagging, and wider GTM resources.

The useful lesson is not “multiply account coverage by four.”

It is that bounded service design can release capacity when four controls exist:

- **Admission:** only material and influenceable work enters.
- **Co-ownership:** the account team contributes context, access, decisions, and follow-through.
- **Progression:** the sprint targets an observable account or buying-group change, not content output.
- **Exit:** the team stops, demotes, recovers, or hands back deliberately.

Remove those controls and the same model produces more activity, more reactive requests, and less strategic attention.

This is how I want to use public ABM cases: not as recipes, but as experiments against a coherent operating system.

Recognize what works.

Separate evidence from advocacy.

Name the conditions.

Then adapt the mechanism.

**Copy the governance, not the number.**

Source: public *Let's talk ABM* interview with Celia Slack. The coverage comparison is source-reported and unaudited; the critique and four-control model are my analysis.
