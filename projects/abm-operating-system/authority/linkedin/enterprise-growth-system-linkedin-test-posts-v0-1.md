# Enterprise Growth System: 10 LinkedIn Test Posts

**Purpose**: Test whether Heads of Marketing in large enterprises engage with the Enterprise Growth System when it is taught through recognizable management tensions rather than a conventional framework explanation.

**Evidence boundary**: The posts explain an original internal framework that is still in draft. They do not claim live performance validation, official HP Inc. ownership, or customer results.

**Editorial hypothesis**: `recognizable problem → uncomfortable reframe → model principle → management decision`

---

## Test 1 — The Campaign Factory

**Format**: Manifesto / category reframe<br>
**Primary test**: Does the operating-model diagnosis resonate?

### Post

Many enterprise marketing problems arrive disguised as campaign requests.

“We need an ABM campaign.”

“We need more executive content.”

“We need to activate these 200 accounts.”

The request sounds tactical. The failure usually is not.

The team has not agreed:

- which commercial state should change;
- which accounts deserve which level of investment;
- which buying group matters;
- who owns the next move;
- or what evidence would cause the team to continue, adapt or stop.

So marketing does what marketing can do.

It produces a campaign.

The result may be attractive, on time and completely disconnected from a commercial decision.

That is why I have been working on an Enterprise Growth System—not another campaign framework.

Its starting point is simple:

**Choose the growth motion before choosing the marketing activity.**

Market Demand, Account-based Demand, 1:few ABM, 1:1 Strategic ABM and Customer Expansion are different jobs.

They need different inputs, owners, investment levels and measures.

More activity cannot repair a missing operating decision.

Sometimes the bravest response to a campaign request is:

**“Before we build it, what exactly are we trying to move?”**

## Test 2 — The Three Labels

**Format**: One diagram, one decision<br>
**Primary test**: Is the tier/motion/status distinction useful enough to save?

### Post

Three labels walk into an account review:

**Tier 1. 1:1 ABM. Hot.**

Everyone nods.

Unfortunately, the three labels answer three different questions.

**Tier** asks:<br>
How important is this account relative to the rest of the portfolio?

**Motion** asks:<br>
What operating model and investment intensity fit the account now?

**Status** asks:<br>
What is currently happening inside that motion?

A Tier-1 account can be:

- in Account-based Demand because evidence is still shallow;
- in 1:1 ABM because a growth thesis, access and commitment exist;
- in Customer Expansion because value realization is the real job;
- or on hold because the organization lacks capacity.

Collapsing all three labels creates a very expensive illusion:

importance becomes readiness,
readiness becomes activity,
and activity becomes “progress.”

My rule:

**Tier determines importance. Motion determines the operating contract. Status determines the next decision.**

If your portfolio review uses one label to do all three jobs, the problem is not the dashboard.

It is the decision architecture behind it.

## Test 3 — The 1:1 ABM VIP Lounge

**Format**: Growth Court<br>
**Primary test**: Does a sharper definition of 1:1 ABM attract or repel the right audience?

### Post

**THE GROWTH COURT**

Case #001:<br>
“Our most valuable accounts belong in 1:1 ABM.”

**Verdict: Guilty of confusing importance with readiness.**

A valuable account deserves attention.

It does not automatically deserve the deepest and most expensive operating model.

Before admitting an account to 1:1 Strategic ABM, I would want five things:

1. A testable growth thesis.
2. A realistic path into the buying group.
3. Active commitment from the account team.
4. Confidence that the business can deliver the proposed value.
5. Enough orchestration capacity to follow through.

Without these conditions, “1:1 ABM” often becomes a VIP lounge:

beautiful account plans, bespoke content, executive names on slides—and no credible path to progression.

The better decision may be to keep the account in a lighter motion:

observe, research, build relevance and wait for stronger evidence.

That is not neglect.

It is portfolio discipline.

**Strategic importance earns review. It does not bypass readiness.**

## Test 4 — The Intent Alarm

**Format**: Enterprise Growth Clinic<br>
**Primary test**: Does the signal-to-action rule create practical value?

### Post

**ENTERPRISE GROWTH CLINIC**

The patient: a Tier-1 account.

The symptom: an intent spike.

The prescribed treatment:<br>
“Sales should contact them today.”

My diagnosis: **signal fever**.

Intent is evidence that something may be happening.

It is not evidence that:

- the right people are involved;
- the topic relates to a funded priority;
- the account wants contact;
- your offer is relevant;
- or a sales intervention is the safest next move.

Before turning a signal into action, I use eight checks:

relevance, specificity, recency, source confidence, role coverage, confirmation from other signals, alternative explanations and relationship risk.

Only then comes the operational question:

**Who owns the next-best useful action?**

Sometimes that action is outreach.

Sometimes it is research, content, account-team validation or simply watching for a second signal.

Signals are not instructions.

They are invitations to make a better decision.

Automating the reaction before improving the interpretation does not create speed.

It industrializes guesswork.

## Test 5 — One Engaged Lead

**Format**: Boardroom dialogue<br>
**Primary test**: Does the buying-group perspective trigger internal sharing?

### Post

CMO: “The account is highly engaged.”

Sales: “Who is engaged?”

Marketing: “One person downloaded four assets and attended a webinar.”

Sales: “Can they influence the decision?”

Marketing: “We don’t know.”

Sales: “Who else is involved?”

Marketing: “We don’t know.”

CMO: “Then the account is not highly engaged. One person is.”

That distinction matters.

Complex enterprise decisions are not made by leads.

They are made by buying groups with different goals, risks and influence:

the initiator, champion, technical evaluator, economic buyer, procurement, implementation owner—and sometimes the quiet blocker nobody has mapped.

Counting engaged contacts can tell us that activity occurred.

It cannot tell us whether the buying group is progressing.

The more useful questions are:

- Are the necessary roles covered?
- Is influence concentrated in one relationship?
- Is information moving between participants?
- Is consensus growing—or are we repeatedly engaging the same friendly contact?

**A lead can respond. A buying group has to move.**

That is a different measurement problem—and a different marketing job.

## Test 6 — The Content Brief That Arrived Too Early

**Format**: Short story / execution-loop lesson<br>
**Primary test**: Does the readiness-gate idea feel fresh rather than bureaucratic?

### Post

The brief looked excellent.

Target account. Executive audience. Personalized storyline. Premium format.

One problem:

Nobody could answer what should happen after the executive consumed it.

There was no account-team commitment.

No agreed buying-group gap.

No relationship owner.

No next action.

No rule for what a positive, negative or absent response would trigger.

The content was not bad.

It had simply arrived before the operating system was ready for it.

In the Enterprise Growth System, content sits inside an execution loop:

commercial priority → intelligence and hypothesis → orchestration → content and experience → execution → learning.

Skip the first three steps and personalization becomes expensive decoration.

The readiness question is not:

**“Do we have enough data to make this personal?”**

It is:

**“Do we have enough alignment to make the response useful?”**

Content does not create orchestration.

Good orchestration gives content a commercial job.

## Test 7 — Demotion Is Not Failure

**Format**: Contrarian management rule<br>
**Primary test**: Does the resource-allocation angle produce executive discussion?

### Post

Imagine presenting this result to the executive team:

“This quarter, we removed six accounts from 1:1 ABM.”

It sounds like failure.

It may be one of the healthiest decisions the program has made.

Perhaps the trigger disappeared.

Perhaps the growth thesis did not survive customer evidence.

Perhaps access to the buying group never materialized.

Perhaps the account team could not commit.

Perhaps customer health made expansion irresponsible.

Keeping those accounts in the deepest motion would protect the dashboard—and waste scarce capacity.

A mature growth system must be able to say:

- continue;
- adapt;
- hold;
- move to a lighter motion;
- or stop.

If every account only moves “forward,” you probably do not have a learning system.

You have a one-way theatre of success.

**Demotion can be progress when it protects resources and improves the next decision.**

The question is not how many accounts remain in the premium program.

The question is whether each account still earns the operating intensity it receives.

## Test 8 — Five Motions, Five Scorecards

**Format**: Measurement myth<br>
**Primary test**: Does the motion-specific measurement principle earn saves?

### Post

“How much pipeline did it create?”

A legitimate question.

And sometimes the wrong first question.

Consider five different growth jobs:

1. Building relevance in a market.
2. Detecting movement across selected accounts.
3. Progressing a shared change hypothesis in a small cluster.
4. Developing one strategic account.
5. Expanding realized value inside an existing customer.

Why would all five be judged by the same scorecard?

Market Demand may need evidence of audience and market movement.

Account-based Demand needs interpretable account and buying-group signals.

1:few and 1:1 ABM need hypothesis, relationship and opportunity progression.

Customer Expansion needs adoption, value realization, retention and white-space evidence.

Commercial outcomes still matter.

But forcing every motion to prove itself through immediate pipeline creates two bad behaviors:

early-stage work overclaims revenue,
and deep account work hides behind activity.

My measurement rule:

**Measure each motion by its mandate—then connect progression to commercial outcomes.**

One dashboard can create visibility.

One KPI cannot create truth.

## Test 9 — The Martech Orchestra

**Format**: Analogy / myth correction<br>
**Primary test**: Does the technology stance differentiate the model?

### Post

A company buys an orchestra.

The best instruments.<br>
Perfect acoustics.<br>
Every musician connected to the same digital score.

There is only one omission:

Nobody agreed what piece to play.

That is how many enterprise growth stacks are implemented.

CRM, intent, automation, content, advertising, sales engagement and analytics are connected.

But the organization still lacks shared answers to:

- Which growth motion owns this account?
- What evidence justifies the investment?
- Who owns the commercial decision?
- What must happen before activation?
- What response changes the plan?
- When do we stop?

Technology can move data.

It cannot negotiate an operating contract between marketing, sales, customer success, experts and leadership.

The sequence matters:

**decision → workflow → governance → data → technology**

Reverse it and the stack scales ambiguity.

Get it right and technology becomes genuinely powerful: not because it “orchestrates” the business, but because it supports decisions the business has already learned how to make.

Tools can amplify an orchestra.

They cannot choose the music.

## Test 10 — Put AI in the Passenger Seat

**Format**: AI point of view / series finale<br>
**Primary test**: Does the human-decision boundary create a distinctive AI position?

### Post

I want AI deeply embedded in enterprise growth.

Just not in the driver’s seat.

AI can help:

- summarize market and account evidence;
- identify missing buying-group roles;
- compare signals and alternative explanations;
- prepare account-review questions;
- draft modular content from approved context;
- detect stale assumptions;
- and preserve learning across teams.

But AI should not autonomously decide:

- that intent equals purchase readiness;
- that a relationship is strong;
- that an opportunity stage should change;
- that an executive should be contacted;
- that an account deserves more investment;
- or that customer risk is acceptable.

Those decisions combine evidence with responsibility, trust, politics, capacity and consequences.

The useful boundary is not “human versus AI.”

It is:

**AI prepares evidence and options. Accountable humans make consequential commercial decisions.**

Before building an AI growth copilot, I would first ask:

Can the organization explain its motions, gates, evidence classes, decision rights and stop rules without AI?

If not, the model will not fix the ambiguity.

It will answer it—confidently, quickly and at scale.

## Test Design

Do not judge the series only by likes. Track:

| Signal | What it may indicate |
|---|---|
| Saves | The distinction or decision rule has reusable value. |
| Shares and tagged colleagues | The post names an internal tension people want others to see. |
| Comments from target roles | The idea attracts the intended audience rather than broad marketing engagement. |
| Dwell time / expansion | The hook earns attention and the structure sustains it. |
| Profile views and relevant connection requests | The series builds authority around the model. |
| Direct messages with concrete cases | The framework invites application, not only agreement. |
| Disagreement from experienced operators | The position is specific enough to test and refine. |

Review after posts 3, 6 and 10. Compare formats and propositions separately; do not infer model validity from LinkedIn engagement.

## Content Taste Gate

- **Share Test**: strongest candidates are Tests 2, 4, 5, 7 and 9 because they provide portable distinctions or language for internal conversations.
- **Onlyness Test**: the proprietary motion, readiness, evidence and decision architecture differentiates the series. Onlyness will become stronger when Rolf adds one genuine observation, case or scar to the best-performing formats.
- **Slop check**: no generic inspiration hooks, faux “unpopular opinions,” engagement-farming questions or broad listicle conclusions.
- **Publication status**: suitable for controlled testing after a human voice check; not evidence that the Enterprise Growth System is validated in practice.
