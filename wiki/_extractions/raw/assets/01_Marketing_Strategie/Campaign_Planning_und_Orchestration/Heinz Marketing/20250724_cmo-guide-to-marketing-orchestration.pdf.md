Strategic Alignment and Planning
Strategic alignment enables a company to efficiently use resources, forecast outcomes, and
maintain consistency in time to market. In contrast, a misaligned company experiences resource
waste, unclear responsibilities, poor accountability, and inconsistent, low-quality outputs that fail
to meet objectives.
Here are the tools to support strategic alignment and planning:
Goal frameworks: Establish clear, measurable objectives that align with overall business
goals. Utilize frameworks like OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) or SMART goals (Specific,
Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) to ensure every team understands and
strives towards common targets.

Targeting and themes: Define your target audience and the key themes that will resonate
with them. Use data-driven insights to identify customer segments, buyer personas, and
messaging themes that align with your brand and campaign goals.

Campaign Briefs
Campaign briefs act as a guideline for your marketing campaign, outlining the plan and goals for
your strategists, providing your creative team direction, and keeping everyone involved on the
same page.

However, in practice, we have found that different functional groups within a mid-large marketing
department often struggle with execution when they are not aligned on all aspects of the go-to-
market process. We even encounter misalignment around how the brief should be built, what
should be included, how inputs and even milestones are defined, and who is responsible for
each step.
                  Example campaign brief

Bringing all your functional teams together to revise your campaign brief can reveal surprising
insights. We find that teams have differing definitions, duplicate efforts, and missed or
unnecessary steps, impacting the overall alignment on goals and messaging.

Evaluating the campaign brief will likely uncover other areas of opportunity for improvement in
your marketing orchestration. The campaign brief is a great tool for illuminating gaps in goals,
roles, and the workflow.

Here are some tips for thinking about assigning roles:
Responsible - Who is responsible for completing the task (the “doer”)? There may be
multiple R’s for a task but be mindful of how many employees are being assigned to one
task.
Consulted - For the task to be completed correctly, is there someone who needs to supply
further information? They are generally SME’s. Be cognizant of the number of employees
who are being pulled in to consult on a deliverable as this may cause delays. Typically, most
of the C’s are in the planning phase.
Accountable - Who ensures that the task is done on time and correctly? There should only
be one person accountable per task. More than one can cause a conflict in decision-making.
Who has the final authority over the task completion and progressing to the next step?
Informed - Who needs to be made aware of the progression of the project (milestones,
decisions, updates) but isn’t directly completing anything or providing necessary information?
Frequently they will be leadership, but often you will want to inform certain team roles of a
task completion because their task may be dependent on this task’s completion.
A similar, but uniquely different tool is a DACI: Driver, Approver, Contributor, Informed matrix. A
DACI is superior in choice when looking at ongoing process ownership without clear endpoints
and where the steps might vary.
To learn more about the difference between a RACI and a DACI and how to make an informed
choice deciding which to implement, read our blog, DACI vs. RACI.

Meeting Cadence
A meeting cadence helps prevent too few and too many meetings. With a templatized agenda,
every meeting has a purpose, inclusive of inputs (things needed to have a productive meeting)
and outputs (next steps that come from the meeting).

Developing a meeting cadence calendar will provide a central resource for teams to easily see
which meetings happen, how often and who all is included.

Each meeting should be defined with:

Purpose: What do you want to walk away with?
Owner: The person who owns the meeting invite including creating an agenda and assuring
all required attendees are invited and will be present
Attendees: The people required to attend the meeting
Meeting Lead(s): The person(s) responsible for creating the presentation or preparing the
materials and leading the discussion
Scribe: The notetaking including listing next steps/action items and noting follow up
questions
Duration: How long should the meeting last?
Frequency: How often does the meeting occur?

Project Management Tools
Project management tools are the lifeline applications for Marketing Orchestration. These tools
help businesses manage change, plan and track projects, assign and schedule resources, and
improve communication and collaboration. They are used to align all your processes and
resources, essential to enhancing efficiency and therefore increasing profitability. PM tools are
also a means to gain clarity and connection across teams.
Tracking the amount and variability in projects is why it is important to use a Project Management
platform that allows for collaboration and communication. When evaluating a PM platform, here
are some other necessities or things to evaluate:

Templates/frameworks: Defined input into the system. This allows for teams to operate
from the processes to maintain consistency. You can (and should) define roles and
responsibilities directly in the templates.
Issue tracking: Track issues that arise and develop a process in which to handle these. This
allows teams/individuals to log and communicate their feedback throughout the process.
Document management: With a robust review process, it’s important to have everything live
in one place and ideally work as a living document that can be linked within the platform.
Time tracking: This is optional, although highly recommended. The more data you have in
terms of how long tasks take to complete, the better. This will allow better forecasting and
help to prioritize projects.

Building Your Workflow
Now that you understand how your teams fit together and how information flows between them,
you can begin to flesh out how everyone works together. The workflow we are referring to
primarily is for illustrating how to get certain projects to market. Its purpose is to get you to the
simplicity on the other side of complexity.

Here are the main steps for building out your workflow:

Figure out what kinds of work your organization does. Do different kinds of campaigns need
a different route? Do you deal with “always on”, “proactive”, and “reactive” intake scenarios
for projects? Organize the workflow by intake scenarios. It’s important to identify all the types
of work because you will want distinct workflows. The most efficient workflow is one that is
linear. It’s time to reconsider your workflow if it comes out nonlinear.
Define inputs and outputs of each step in your workflow. What do you need to go into the
next step prepared, and what needs to be the result that comes out of taking that step? Not
everything needs a big plan. You may have fast tracks for certain items like wording
changes, version updates, small website text changes, etc. Define rules for these fast tracks
and exceptions.
Find a stakeholder who represents every intake and the steps. Bring these stakeholders
together to discuss the drafted current state of the workflow.

IMPLEMENTING MARKETING ORCHESTRATION
Now that we’ve covered the core concepts of Marketing Orchestration, next we’ll address
common hurdles and questions that may come up during implementation.
Why are things taking so long?
This is by far the most common issue we see among marketing orchestration projects. If you are
continuously asking yourself why it is taking so long to get X project to market (be it a campaign,
asset, or some other resource), it is likely a process problem. The persistence of this problem
means that your process isn’t trackable. You can’t get the status reports you need.

Furthermore, you can’t capacity plan if you don’t know how long things take or why. If your team
frequently mentions a lack of SLAs or problems with timelines, you have this problem. Effective
orchestration processes will give you the data you need to have effective and obtainable
timelines.

Here are a handful of the other knock-on effects of this type of disorganization:
No process integrity – work comes in and is actioned in a haphazard way
Burn-out – non-standardized workflows increase cognitive load and fatigue
Inability to adapt – Effective adaptation demands speed, which you don’t have

Effective marketing orchestration balances out the flexibility needed to adapt to market conditions
with the process integrity needed for your team to not just be sane, but happy. Timelines are an
important part of governance, and your team deserves a solid, dependable process.
How many times do we have to solve this?
Another common symptom is when the same problems come up time after time. They may seem
slightly different each time, giving the illusion of progress, but at their core they are dealing with
the same old problems. There is usually an “ah-ha” moment when a leader connects the dots
between all their missed deadlines and team complaints.
If you solve problems as they come up, be on the lookout for similarities between them and
overall patterns. You will eventually realize that you have been playing whack-a-mole for years.
Orchestration means every team working together in concert, and thus one team’s problem
impacts the entire ensemble. To break the band-aid loop, you need to recognize the
commonalities and solve the root cause.

PUTTING IT ALL TOGETHER
So, how do you start improving your Marketing Orchestration? The action plan is simple in theory
but takes time and work. Marketing Orchestration isn’t a “set it and forget it” type of approach. It
requires testing, gathering feedback, and optimizing.

Step 1: Testing
This might look different depending on the size of your organization and what your workflow
looks like. Remember that Marketing Orchestration covers campaign planning all the way
through execution, the final product.

To effectively structure your test:

Define Variables: Identify what elements of your marketing campaigns can be adjusted. This
might include messaging, channels, timing, or audience segments.
Control Elements: Determine what needs to remain consistent to ensure valid results. This
might be your core message, brand guidelines, or budget.
Set Up Systems: Implement systems to track and measure the impact of changes. This
could include analytics tools, customer feedback mechanisms, and performance metrics.

Option 1: Test a portion of the process, for example content production.

Benefits
Efficiency: Allows for quicker identification and resolution of issues, enabling teams to
iterate rapidly
Resource Management: Requires fewer resources initially, make it more feasible for
smaller-scale testing or organizations with limited resources
Focused Analysis: Enables teams to concentrate on specific areas, facilitating deeper
analysis and understanding of potential challenges

Considerations
Limited Insight: May overlook broader system issues that only emerge when testing the
entire process
Incomplete Picture: Without testing the entire process, the overall effectiveness and
efficiency of the process remain uncertain until the whole process is tested

Option 2: Test the entire process from start to finish.
Pros
Comprehensive Understanding: Provides a holistic view of the process, uncovering issues
that may only manifest when all components are integrated
Real-world Simulation: Mimics real-world scenarios more accurately, revealing how
different parts of the process impact each other
Quality Assurance: Ensures that the process functions seamlessly from end to end,
enhancing overall quality and experience
Cons
Resource Intensive: Requires more time, effort, and resources upfront, potentially delaying
implementation or iterations
Complexity: Managing and coordinating testing across multiple segments simultaneously
can be challenging, increasing the risk of errors or oversights
Difficulty in Isolation: Identifying and addressing specific issues within the larger process
may be more complex, making it harder to pinpoint root causes

Ultimately, the choice between testing part of the process first or testing the entire process
depends on factors such as the complexity of the process, available resources, and the desired
level of insight and assurance.

Step 2: Launch
The key here is testing never ends so do not wait to launch. The process won’t be perfect and it’s
built to be iterative. That’s why you have built in feedback loops and have a process for resolving
issues while the process is in play.

Step 3: Optimize
Optimization doesn’t stop either. It will take place during testing but also once the process is
launched. A major tip with optimization is not to reboot. Unless there is a catastrophic issue that
would cause things to come to a halt, although very unlikely.

The Marketing Orchestration approach outlined above enables your teams to adjust on the fly.
Rebooting should be unnecessary.
